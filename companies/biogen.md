## History: 1995-2025

### 1995-2002: The Avonex engine
Founded in Geneva in 1978 by academic scientists including future Nobel laureates Walter Gilbert and Phillip Sharp, Biogen spent its first two decades as a licensing-driven pioneer of recombinant biology. Its transformation into a fully integrated company came with the 1996 FDA approval of Avonex (interferon beta-1a) for relapsing multiple sclerosis — the best-selling MS therapy of its era, which defined Biogen's identity as a neurology specialist built on a single dominant franchise.

### 2003-2012: The IDEC merger and the Tysabri crisis
In 2003 Biogen merged with San Diego-based IDEC Pharmaceuticals — maker, with Genentech, of the pioneering cancer antibody Rituxan (rituximab) — in a stock deal valued around $6.8 billion, creating Biogen Idec. The next flagship, Tysabri (natalizumab), was approved for MS in November 2004 and voluntarily withdrawn in February 2005 after cases of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a rare, often fatal brain infection. Relaunched in 2006 under a strict risk-management program, it grew into a multi-billion-dollar product (Biogen bought Elan's share in 2013), while the anti-CD20 royalty stream from Rituxan — and later Ocrevus — became a quiet, durable earnings engine.

### 2013-2019: Tecfidera's boom and the aducanumab gamble
Oral Tecfidera (dimethyl fumarate), launched in 2013, was among the fastest drug launches in biotech history, peaking above $4 billion a year and making Biogen the world's leading MS company. In 2017 it spun off its hemophilia business as Bioverativ (bought by Sanofi for $11.6 billion in 2018) and paid Forward Pharma $1.25 billion to shore up Tecfidera IP. December 2016 brought Spinraza (nusinersen), the first treatment for spinal muscular atrophy, licensed from Ionis — a landmark for antisense medicine and a new billion-dollar franchise. Meanwhile Biogen doubled down on Alzheimer's: its anti-amyloid antibody aducanumab failed a futility analysis in March 2019, only for the company to revive the program that October, arguing high-dose data from one of two phase 3 trials was positive.

### 2020-2022: The cliff and the Aduhelm debacle
In June 2020 a US court invalidated Tecfidera's key patent in litigation with Mylan; generics flooded in that August and US Tecfidera revenue collapsed. Biogen steered patients toward follow-on fumarate Vumerity, but the MS franchise entered structural decline. Then came Aduhelm (aducanumab): the FDA granted accelerated approval in June 2021 against the near-unanimous advice of its advisory committee — three members resigned in protest. The launch, priced initially at $56,000/year, failed; in April 2022 Medicare restricted coverage to clinical trials, effectively ending commercial prospects. The saga triggered congressional investigation of FDA-Biogen interactions, billions in write-offs, deep cost cuts, and the exit of CEO Michel Vounatsos; former Sanofi chief Christopher Viehbacher arrived as CEO in November 2022. That year Biogen also sold about $2.3 billion of its Samsung Bioepis stake back to Samsung Biologics. Aduhelm was formally discontinued in January 2024, rights returning to Neurimmune.

### 2023-2025: The Viehbacher reset
Viehbacher's playbook: shrink the cost base, diversify beyond MS, and rebuild credibility in Alzheimer's. Leqembi (lecanemab), the Eisai-partnered anti-amyloid antibody, won full FDA approval in July 2023 — the first disease-modifying Alzheimer's therapy with traditional approval — though uptake was gradual (global in-market sales ~$510 million in calendar 2025, split 50/50 with Eisai, who leads the program). Biogen paid $7.3 billion for Reata Pharmaceuticals in 2023, gaining Skyclarys (omaveloxolone), the first Friedreich's ataxia treatment, and launched Zurzuvae (zuranolone, with Sage Therapeutics) for postpartum depression after the FDA rejected the broader major-depression indication. The "Fit for Growth" restructuring (announced July 2023) delivered roughly $1 billion in gross savings by end-2025 with about 1,400 net job cuts, and in 2024 Biogen added HI-Bio for $1.15 billion (felzartamab, immunology). FY2025 revenue of $9.89 billion marked the first annual growth in several years — growth products rose 19%, offsetting MS erosion — though a Q3 2025 court judgment for Genentech over past Tysabri royalties produced a notable one-time charge.

## Current strategic bets

Biogen's 2025-2026 strategy pivots from the declining MS legacy toward four pillars: Alzheimer's, rare disease, immunology, and — new in 2026 — complement biology spanning nephrology and retina.

The boldest move is the acquisition of Apellis Pharmaceuticals (announced March 31, closed May 14, 2026): $41/share cash (~$5.6 billion) plus a CVR up to $4/share tied to Syfovre sales. It brings two marketed C3 complement inhibitors — Syfovre (geographic atrophy) and Empaveli (rare kidney diseases and PNH) — with $689 million combined 2025 revenue and mid-to-high-teens expected growth, financed partly with a $2 billion term loan; management expects EPS accretion from 2027 and ≥$250 million in synergies. In June 2026 Biogen agreed to acquire RayThera, a San Diego immunology small-molecule startup, for up to $1 billion, and in February 2025 it paid Stoke Therapeutics $165 million upfront (up to $385 million in milestones) for ex-North America rights to zorevunersen in Dravet syndrome. No major China-originated in-licensing deals to date — Biogen's BD has centered on US biotech.

In Alzheimer's, the near-term bets are convenience and prevention: Leqembi Iqlik subcutaneous maintenance launched October 2025 (FDA decision on subcutaneous initiation due May 24, 2026), with the AHEAD 3-45 preclinical-AD trial reading out later this decade. Diranersen (BIIB080), a tau-targeting antisense oligonucleotide partnered with Ionis, reported Phase 2 CELIA topline results May 14, 2026: the primary dose-response endpoint was missed, but tau-pathology reductions across all doses and pre-specified analyses showing slowed cognitive decline — a first for a tau-directed agent in a randomized trial — led Biogen to commit to registrational development.

Management frames this as "one company declining, one growing": 2026 guidance calls for revenue down mid-single digits as MS erodes, with the growth portfolio, Apellis assets, and a dense run of registrational readouts carrying the re-rating case.

## Key risks

- **MS franchise decline**: ~$4.1B of FY2025 MS revenue eroding — Tecfidera fell 29.7% in 2025 to $680M on EU generics; Tysabri biosimilar erosion is worldwide. 2026 total revenue guided down mid-single digits.
- **Concentration**: Tysabri and Spinraza each exceeded 10% of 2025 revenue; Spinraza faces Zolgensma/Evrysdi competition; Leqembi economics are shared 50/50 with Eisai, who controls development.
- **Leqembi execution**: uptake constrained by diagnosis and infusion logistics; competition from Eli Lilly's Kisunla (donanemab).
- **IRA / pricing**: Medicare Part D redesign pressure; Spinraza, Tysabri, and Skyclarys are plausible future negotiation candidates; US pricing/tariff policy uncertainty.
- **Balance sheet**: ~$5.4B Apellis deal debt-financed ($2B term loan); ~$0.85 EPS dilution in 2026; synergies required for 2027 accretion.
- **Litigation**: Q3 2025 judgment for Genentech on past Tysabri royalties; history of IP and regulatory-scrutiny episodes (Aduhelm congressional report, Forward Pharma).
- **Pipeline binarity**: multiple registrational readouts 2026-2027 (litifilimab, felzartamab, zorevunersen, salanersen); diranersen advances despite a missed Phase 2 primary endpoint — a regulatory risk.
