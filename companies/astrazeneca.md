## Pipeline (clinical-stage)

AstraZeneca's $80B-by-2030 ambition depends on a pipeline broad enough to outrun the Farxiga cliff that is already here and the Tagrisso and Calquence cliffs that arrive around 2032, and 2025-2026 is when that pipeline starts landing. The nearest-term wave is cardiometabolic and rare disease: a first-in-class blood-pressure pill approved in May 2026, an oral weight-loss pill moving into Phase III, and three Alexion rare-disease medicines sitting with regulators. The oncology bets are larger and slower — bispecific antibodies designed to succeed Imfinzi as the immunotherapy backbone, a more selective successor to Lynparza, and antibody-drug conjugates that deliver chemotherapy directly to tumour cells — and most of them, like baxdrostat and the oral GLP-1, were bought or licensed rather than discovered in-house.

## History: 1995-2025

### 1995-1999: Two heritages converge
AstraZeneca's roots run through Astra AB, founded in Södertälje, Sweden in 1913, and Zeneca Group plc, the pharmaceutical business demerged from Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) in 1993. By the late 1990s Astra owned Losec (omeprazole), then the world's best-selling drug, while Zeneca brought oncology assets including Nolvadex (tamoxifen) and Zoladex (goserelin). On 6 April 1999 the two combined in one of the largest European mergers to that date (roughly $67B combined value, est.), creating an Anglo-Swedish company headquartered in London with R&D anchored in both countries.

### 1999-2011: Primary-care blockbusters and the biologics pivot
The 2000s were powered by mass-market franchises: Nexium (esomeprazole), the follow-on to Losec; Crestor (rosuvastatin) in cholesterol; Seroquel (quetiapine) in psychiatry; and Symbicort in respiratory. But the decade also seeded later trouble: Iressa (gefitinib) stumbled in unselected lung cancer patients in 2002-2004 before its EGFR-targeted rationale was understood, the anticoagulant Exanta was withdrawn on liver safety, and in 2010 the company paid $520M to settle US claims over off-label Seroquel marketing. The strategic pivot of the era was biologics: Cambridge Antibody Technology was acquired in 2006, then MedImmune in 2007 for about $15.2B — widely criticized as expensive at the time, but the source of later antibody capabilities behind Imfinzi, Fasenra and Beyfortus.

### 2012-2016: Patent cliff, near-takeover, and the Soriot turnaround
Between 2012 and 2016 AstraZeneca fell off one of the industry's steepest patent cliffs: Seroquel (2012), Nexium (US generics 2015) and Crestor (US 2016) wiped out well over $10B of annual revenue, with sales sliding from about $33B in 2011 toward roughly $23B by 2016-2017. Pascal Soriot arrived from Roche in October 2012 and bet the company on oncology and pipeline science. In May 2014 the board rejected Pfizer's final takeover proposal of about £69B (~$117B) — then the largest attempted pharma deal ever — with Soriot famously promising investors $45B of revenue by 2023. Rebuilding moves followed: full buyout of the Bristol-Myers Squibb diabetes alliance (2014), a $4B deal for 55% of Acerta Pharma (2015) bringing acalabrutinib, first approvals for Lynparza (2014) and Tagrisso (2015), and relocation of headquarters and research to Cambridge.

### 2017-2020: Oncology inflection and the COVID vaccine
The PACIFIC trial (2017) established Imfinzi in stage III lung cancer, offsetting the high-profile failure of the MYSTIC trial the same year. Partnering became a signature: Lynparza was co-developed with Merck & Co. from 2017 (deal worth up to $8.5B), and in 2019 AstraZeneca paid up to $6.9B to share Daiichi Sankyo's Enhertu, followed in 2020 by up to $6B for datopotamab deruxtecan (now Datroway). Farxiga's DAPA-HF and DAPA-CKD trials expanded SGLT2 inhibitors far beyond diabetes. In 2020 the company co-developed the Oxford COVID-19 vaccine (Vaxzevria), supplying billions of doses at no profit during the pandemic — a public-health success shadowed by dosing-data controversies, rare clotting events and EU supply litigation. In December 2020 it announced its largest-ever acquisition: Alexion Pharmaceuticals for $39B.

### 2021-2025: Rare disease scale and a $80B ambition
The Alexion deal closed in July 2021 (purchase consideration fair value $41.1B), adding the Soliris/Ultomiris complement franchise and a rare-disease growth engine. Revenue climbed from about $37B in 2021 to $58.7B in 2025 — comfortably surpassing the $45B pledge made during the Pfizer defense. Bolt-ons filled the pipeline: CinCor (baxdrostat, 2023, ~$1.3B upfront), Gracell (cell therapy, $1.2B) and Fusion (radioconjugates, $2.4B) in 2024, EsoBiotec (in vivo CAR-T, ~$1B) in 2025. The period's biggest shock came from China, historically AZ's emerging-markets stronghold: China president Leon Wang was detained in October 2024 amid probes into insurance fraud, illegal drug importation and data breaches; in November 2025 Chinese prosecutors indicted the China subsidiary, Wang and another former employee. In 2024 Soriot set a $80B-by-2030 revenue ambition; 2025 closed with 16 positive Phase III readouts, 43 major-region approvals, and a decisive tilt toward the United States.

## Current strategic bets

AstraZeneca's stated ambition is $80B in total revenue by 2030, with roughly half generated in the US. Three moves define 2025-2026. First, an American re-anchoring: a $50B US manufacturing and R&D investment pledge (July 2025), headlined by a $4.5B drug-substance facility in Virginia for the weight-management and metabolic portfolio; an October 2025 agreement with the Trump administration granting a three-year exemption from Section 232 pharmaceutical tariffs in exchange for most-favored-nation pricing to Medicaid, up-to-80% direct-to-consumer discounts and participation in TrumpRx; and a shareholder-approved direct listing on the NYSE. Second, a deliberate push into cardiometabolic and weight management: oral GLP-1 (AZD5004, licensed from Eccogene), oral PCSK9 (AZD0780), and baxdrostat, the aldosterone synthase inhibitor that delivered positive BaxHTN Phase III data in resistant hypertension (US PDUFA in 2026). Third, continued oncology depth — ADCs and next-generation endocrine therapy — despite the looming Farxiga cliff. In China, AstraZeneca is doubling down despite legal overhang, with a $2.5B Beijing R&D hub (March 2025) and a pledge exceeding 100 billion yuan by 2030.

## Key risks

- **Farxiga cliff, now:** ~$8.4B product (2025) with US generics approved April 2026 and China volume-based procurement from Q1 2026; Lynparza (~$3.3B) follows later this decade (est. ~2028).
- **2030s oncology cliff:** Tagrisso ($7.25B) and Calquence (~$3.5B est.) face LOE around 2032 (est.) — the $80B ambition assumes the pipeline outruns these.
- **US concentration:** ~42-43% of revenue from the US and rising by design; exposure to IRA Medicare price negotiation (Farxiga among selected drugs), MFN/Medicaid pricing concessions, and any future tariff regime after the three-year exemption lapses.
- **China legal and pricing risk:** second-largest market (~$6.6B, est. definitions vary); the company and former executives were indicted in Nov 2025 (illegal data collection, illegal importation; individuals also insurance fraud); VBP and NRDL pricing pressure persists; US securities class action tied to disclosure of the probes.
- **Rare-disease erosion:** Soliris biosimilars (since 2024) erode faster than Ultomiris conversion offsets; Alexion franchise carries high per-patient pricing exposure.
- **Partnered-economics drag:** growing share of revenue from profit-shared products (Enhertu, Datroway, Tezspire, Lynparza) structurally dilutes gross margin.
