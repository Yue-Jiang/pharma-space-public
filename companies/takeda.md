## History: 1995-2025

### Roots and the golden blockbusters (1781-2008)
Takeda traces to 1781, when Chobei Takeda I began selling traditional medicines in Osaka's Doshomachi district — making it one of the world's oldest pharmaceutical companies. By the 1990s it was Japan's largest drugmaker, powered by four global blockbusters largely partnered in the US: Prevacid (lansoprazole) for acid reflux, Actos (pioglitazone) for type 2 diabetes, Blopress (candesartan) for hypertension, and leuprorelin for prostate cancer, several sold through the TAP Pharmaceuticals joint venture with Abbott (dissolved 2008). This era made Takeda enormously profitable but dangerously dependent on a handful of small molecules heading toward patent expiry.

### Buying a way out of the cliff (2008-2014)
Facing loss of exclusivity on Prevacid (2009) and Actos (2012), Takeda went shopping. In 2008 it paid $8.8 billion for Millennium Pharmaceuticals, gaining the proteasome inhibitor Velcade (bortezomib) and a Boston oncology hub that remains the center of its R&D today. In 2011 it bought Swiss-based Nycomed for roughly $13.7 billion, adding European and emerging-markets reach. The Actos franchise ended painfully: beyond generics, bladder-cancer litigation produced a headline $9 billion punitive award in 2014 (later drastically reduced) and a ~$2.4 billion US settlement in 2015. Revenue stagnated and the company looked slow-moving next to global peers.

### The Weber transformation (2014-2018)
In 2014 Takeda recruited Christophe Weber from GlaxoSmithKline — its first non-Japanese leader, becoming CEO in 2015. Weber globalized management, concentrated R&D on four areas (oncology, GI, neuroscience, rare diseases plus vaccines), and kept dealmaking: the $5.2 billion acquisition of Ariad Pharmaceuticals (2017) brought Iclusig (ponatinib) and Alunbrig (brigatinib), and TiGenix (2018, ~€520M) added the cell therapy Alofisel. Meanwhile Entyvio (vedolizumab), launched 2014 from the Millennium legacy, grew into the company's flagship.

### The Shire mega-bet and deleveraging (2018-2023)
In May 2018 Takeda agreed to buy Irish-headquartered Shire for $62 billion (closing January 2019) — the largest foreign acquisition ever by a Japanese company, roughly $80 billion including debt. Shire, which had itself absorbed Baxalta for $32 billion in 2016, brought rare diseases (Takhzyro, Elaprase, hemophilia), the plasma-derived therapies business (Gammagard), and the ADHD blockbuster Vyvanse (lisdexamfetamine). The deal doubled revenue past $30 billion but loaded on ~5x leverage; Takeda spent the next five years divesting ~$13 billion of non-core assets — Xiidra to Novartis for $5.3 billion, its Japanese consumer-health unit to Blackstone for ~$2.3 billion — to work debt down toward its 2x net-debt/EBITDA target. Pipeline setbacks accumulated in 2023: lung-cancer drug Exkivity (mobocertinib) was withdrawn after a failed confirmatory trial, Alofisel failed Phase 3, and the US application for dengue vaccine Qdenga was withdrawn. The heaviest blow was scheduled: Vyvanse, which had grown past $3 billion a year (est.), lost US exclusivity in August 2023 and generics erased most of that revenue within two years.

### Transition and renewal (2023-2026)
Takeda answered the cliff with cost discipline and late-stage bets. A multiyear restructuring targeting ~¥300 billion (~$2B) in annualized savings trimmed thousands of roles; soticlestat (epilepsy) failed Phase 3 in 2024 and the cell-therapy platform was wound down in 2025, driving impairments. But the licensing engine delivered: fruquintinib (Fruzaqla, from China's HutchMed, $400M upfront, 2023) became a fast-growing colorectal-cancer launch; zasocitinib (TYK2 inhibitor, from Nimbus for $4 billion upfront in December 2022), rusfertide (Protagonist, $300M upfront, January 2024) and elritercept (Keros, $200M upfront, December 2024) filled the late-stage pipeline alongside home-grown oveporexton. FY2025 (ended March 2026) revenue was ~¥4.5 trillion (~$28.3 billion core), down slightly on Vyvanse erosion, and a jury verdict in the Amitiza (lubiprostone) pay-for-delay antitrust case (May 2026) forced a ¥402.5 billion provision that nearly wiped out reported operating profit. In June 2026 Julie Kim — who joined via Shire and ran the US business — succeeded the retiring Weber, becoming Takeda's first woman and first American CEO, inheriting three imminent launches; oveporexton was approved as Orzeyful in August 2026.

## Current strategic bets

Takeda's 2025-2026 story is a handoff from the Vyvanse-eroded portfolio to what management calls the most robust late-stage pipeline in company history, with $10-20 billion in combined peak-sales potential across six assets (company guidance, JPM 2025). Three launches anchor the next 18 months. Orzeyful (oveporexton), an oral orexin receptor 2 agonist and the first drug to treat the underlying cause of narcolepsy type 1, was FDA-approved August 5, 2026 (US launch pending DEA scheduling; also approved in China, filed in Japan) — Takeda is positioning it as the start of an orexin franchise (TAK-360, TAK-495 for narcolepsy type 2 and idiopathic hypersomnia). Rusfertide (hepcidin mimetic for polycythemia vera, partnered with Protagonist, which exercised its US profit-share opt-out in April 2026) holds FDA priority review with launch expected H2 2026. Zasocitinib, the $4 billion Nimbus-originated oral TYK2 inhibitor, posted positive Phase 3 psoriasis data in late 2025; filings are underway for a planned 2027 launch against Bristol Myers Squibb's Sotyktu and J&J's icotrokinra.

Behind these sit mezagitamab (anti-CD38, immune thrombocytopenia/IgA nephropathy, Phase 3), fazirsiran (RNAi for alpha-1 antitrypsin liver disease, with Arrowhead, Phase 3), and elritercept (activin inhibitor for anemia in myelodysplastic syndromes/myelofibrosis, Phase 3 RENEW). BD leans on external and China-originated innovation: HutchMed's fruquintinib, an option deal with Ascentage Pharma for third-generation BCR-ABL inhibitor olverembatinib, and a ~$1 billion AI antibody-design pact with Nabla Bio (2025). Modality-wise, Takeda has retreated from cell therapy and doubled down on small molecules, biologics, peptides and RNAi.

Management guidance under Julie Kim frames a "two-horizon" plan: defend core brands (Entyvio, immunoglobulins, Takhzyro) and deliver OPEX savings (~¥300B annualized) in horizon one; drive launch-led growth to a low-to-mid-30s% core operating margin in horizon two.

## Key risks

- **Entyvio concentration and cliff**: ~20% of revenue (¥914.1B in FY2024); biosimilar competition expected around 2031-2032 (est.) — the next big cliff after Vyvanse, with the new launches needing to scale before it hits.
- **Vyvanse tail + legacy erosion**: FY2025 was guided as the last year of major generic headwind, but Japan legacy brands (Azilva et al.) keep eroding.
- **Amitiza antitrust litigation**: May 2026 jury verdict of $884.9M single damages, trebling to ~$2.66B for most classes; Takeda booked a ¥402.5B provision (reported operating profit fell ~98% in FY2025) and is appealing.
- **Launch execution**: three near-simultaneous launches (Orzeyful pending DEA scheduling; rusfertide; zasocitinib) into competitive categories (orexin rivals; TYK2 vs Sotyktu/icotrokinra); pipeline attrition history (Exkivity, Alofisel, soticlestat, cell therapy) shows downside.
- **Pricing/IRA**: US Medicare Part D redesign and negotiation exposure for aging brands, plus recurring Japan price cuts — company cites "drug pricing legislation" headwinds in guidance.
- **Balance sheet**: net debt/adjusted EBITDA 2.6x at FY2025 year-end — manageable but limits mega-M&A; progressive dividend (¥200/share) adds commitment.
