## Pipeline (clinical-stage)

Everything in Merck's pipeline is measured against one date. Keytruda loses US exclusivity in 2028, and it is roughly half the company's revenue, so the pipeline's job is to replace an unrepeatable drug. The answer has two halves: new oncology mechanisms meant to become the next backbone of cancer treatment — a TROP2 ADC, a PD-1/VEGF bispecific, an individualised mRNA cancer vaccine — and a deliberate spread into places Keytruda never went, including the first pill that works like the injected cholesterol biologics, once-weekly HIV treatment, and flu prevention that does not depend on a vaccine. A striking share of it was licensed from Chinese biotechs.

## History: 1995-2025

### 1995–2004: Blockbuster machine and the Vioxx shock
In the mid-1990s Merck was widely regarded as the industry's premier science house, riding statin pioneer Zocor (simvastatin), osteoporosis drug Fosamax (alendronate) and asthma pill Singulair (montelukast). Vioxx (rofecoxib), a COX-2 painkiller launched in 1999, became a multi-billion-seller — until September 2004, when Merck voluntarily withdrew it worldwide after trial data showed elevated heart-attack and stroke risk. The recall vaporized roughly $2.5 billion in annual sales and triggered tens of thousands of lawsuits; Merck settled the bulk for $4.85 billion in 2007 and later paid a $950 million government settlement in 2011. The company also unwound its 1993 diversification into pharmacy benefits, spinning off Medco Health Solutions in 2003.

### 2005–2013: Rebuilding and the Schering-Plough merger
Under CEO Richard Clark, Merck rebuilt from within: Gardasil (HPV vaccine) and Januvia (sitagliptin), both launched in 2006, plus HIV drug Isentress (raltegravir) in 2007. The transformative move came in 2009, when Merck merged with Schering-Plough for about $41 billion — gaining Temodar, Nasonex, the Zetia/Vytorin cholesterol franchise, and, buried in the pipeline, an anti-PD-1 antibody that Schering-Plough had inherited from its 2007 purchase of Organon. That molecule, initially deprioritized, became pembrolizumab. Ken Frazier took over as CEO in 2011, holding R&D spending firm through the 2012 Singulair patent cliff, which erased several billion dollars of revenue.

### 2014–2020: The Keytruda era
Merck raced pembrolizumab through development, and Keytruda won its first FDA approval in September 2014 for melanoma. The decisive bet was pairing it with PD-L1 diagnostics and targeting first-line lung cancer: KEYNOTE-024 (2016) and KEYNOTE-189 (2018) established Keytruda as the backbone of non-small cell lung cancer treatment, leapfrogging Bristol Myers Squibb's Opdivo. Merck pruned elsewhere — selling consumer care to Bayer for $14.2 billion (2014), buying antibiotics maker Cubist for ~$9.5 billion (2015), and writing off most of the $3.85 billion Idenix hepatitis-C acquisition. Global oncology partnerships added Lynparza (AstraZeneca, 2017) and Lenvima (Eisai, 2018), and the ~$1 billion Peloton Therapeutics deal (2019) brought belzutifan.

### 2021–2025: Engineering the post-Keytruda company
Rob Davis became CEO in July 2021, shortly after Merck spun off its women's-health and established-brands business as Organon (June 2021). With Keytruda approaching half of revenue, Merck went on a sustained buying spree: Acceleron for $11.5 billion (2021), delivering PAH drug Winrevair (approved March 2024); Prometheus Biosciences for $10.8 billion (2023), adding TL1A antibody tulisokibart for inflammatory bowel disease; a $4 billion-upfront (up to $22 billion) antibody-drug-conjugate alliance with Daiichi Sankyo (2023); plus Imago, Harpoon, EyeBio and ADC deals with China's Kelun-Biotech. COVID antiviral Lagevrio provided a temporary windfall in 2022. The Gardasil franchise, briefly a ~$9 billion business, collapsed in China from 2024 — full-year 2025 sales fell 39% to $5.2 billion. In 2025 Merck bought Verona Pharma for ~$10 billion (COPD drug Ohtuvayre) and agreed to acquire Cidara Therapeutics for ~$9.2 billion (long-acting flu-prevention antiviral CD388), launched subcutaneous Keytruda Qlex, and announced a $3 billion cost-optimization program — closing 2025 with sales of $65.0 billion, up 1%.

## Current strategic bets

Merck's central strategic problem is that Keytruda — $31.7B in FY2025, roughly 49% of total revenue — loses US exclusivity in 2028. Management frames the transition as "more of a hill than a cliff," resting on three pillars.

First, franchise extension. Keytruda Qlex (pembrolizumab with berahyaluronidase alfa), the subcutaneous version approved and launched in late 2025, injects in minutes rather than a half-hour infusion and is approved across most of the IV label; converting 30-40% of US volume before biosimilars arrive is the goal. Merck is also developing successor mechanisms, notably MK-2010 (LM-299), a PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody licensed from China's LaNova Medicines in late 2024 ($588M upfront, up to $2.7B milestones) as a hedge against ivonescimab-class competitors, and the individualized mRNA cancer vaccine intismeran autogene (V940) with Moderna in Phase 3.

Second, a broad late-stage pipeline Merck values at over $70B in potential mid-2030s revenue. Key assets: sacituzumab tirumotecan (sac-TMT), a TROP2 antibody-drug conjugate from Kelun-Biotech in multiple Phase 3 trials (positive lung and endometrial cancer readouts 2025-26, FDA national priority voucher); Lipfendra (enlicitide), the first oral PCSK9 inhibitor, FDA-approved in 2026 after Phase 3 CORALreef trials showed ~56-60% LDL-cholesterol reductions, with a 14,500-patient outcomes trial ongoing; Winrevair label expansion (HYPERION stopped early for efficacy); tulisokibart in Phase 3 for ulcerative colitis and Crohn's; CD388 (Phase 3 flu prophylaxis, via Cidara); once-weekly oral HIV regimens (islatravir with doravirine, and with Gilead's lenacapavir — positive Phase 3 in 2026); and HRS-5346, an oral Lp(a) inhibitor licensed from Jiangsu Hengrui Pharma (March 2025; $200M upfront, up to $1.77B milestones). China-originated assets (Kelun, LaNova, Hengrui) are now central to the pipeline. The Terns Pharmaceuticals acquisition (~$6.7B, 2026) added an oral BCR::ABL1 inhibitor for chronic myeloid leukemia.

Third, commercial diversification: Ohtuvayre (analyst peak estimates $3-4B), Capvaxive, Enflonsia, and Animal Health ($6.4B, +8%). In early 2026 Merck reorganized Human Health into an Oncology unit and a Specialty, Pharma & Infectious Diseases unit, with the $3B annual cost savings (by end-2027) reinvested in growth areas. FY2026 guidance: $65.5-67.0B.

## Key risks

- **Keytruda LOE 2028**: ~$31.7B (49% of FY2025 revenue) exposed to US biosimilar entry; success hinges on unproven scale of Qlex conversion and pipeline replacement. PD-1/VEGF bispecifics (e.g., ivonescimab) could disrupt the class even earlier.
- **2026 patent cliffs**: Januvia/Janumet (~$2.7B est.) and Bridion (~$1.8B est.) lose US exclusivity in 2026 — a near-term ~$4B+ headwind already reflected in soft FY2026 guidance.
- **Gardasil**: China demand collapse cut franchise sales 39% to $5.2B in 2025 (zero China sales in Q4 2025); domestic Chinese HPV vaccines and Japan softness add structural pressure.
- **Pipeline concentration**: heavy reliance on oncology/ADCs; Daiichi Sankyo ADC program setbacks (e.g., patritumab deruxtecan) illustrate execution risk on multi-billion-dollar upfronts.
- **IRA / pricing**: Januvia was in the first cohort of Medicare-negotiated prices (effective 2026); Keytruda faces potential selection later in the decade; broader US pricing and tariff policy risk (~$200M tariff cost flagged in 2025).
- **Litigation**: ongoing Gardasil product-liability suits (US MDL); legacy Zetia antitrust settlements; routine patent challenges across the portfolio.
