## Pipeline (clinical-stage)

Vertex has one enormous business — cystic fibrosis, still roughly 96% of revenue — and an explicit plan to end that dependence by standing up four or five more franchises. 2026 is the year the plan starts producing products rather than promises: povetacicept for a common kidney disease faces an FDA decision on 30 November, and a $10 billion acquisition of Crinetics adds a rare-endocrine business with one drug already approved. The two biggest long shots are suzetrigine in chronic nerve pain, where Vertex's non-opioid painkiller has yet to prove itself beyond short-term surgical pain, and zimislecel, a one-time islet transplant that left most Phase 1/2 patients insulin-independent.

## History: 1995–2025

### Rational drug design roots and the HIV/HCV era (1989–2011)
Vertex was founded in 1989 in Cambridge, Massachusetts by Joshua Boger, a chemist who left Merck to pursue structure-based "rational" drug design — a founding story chronicled in Barry Werth's *The Billion-Dollar Molecule* (1994). The company went public in 1991 and spent its first two decades as a research-driven, chronically unprofitable biotech. Early fruits included the HIV protease inhibitors amprenavir (Agenerase, 1999) and fosamprenavir (Lexiva, 2003), both partnered with GlaxoSmithKline. The transformative bet of the 2000s was telaprevir (Incivek), a hepatitis C protease inhibitor approved in May 2011. Incivek delivered one of the fastest drug launches in industry history, but the franchise collapsed within three years as Gilead's all-oral, interferon-free Sovaldi regimen made it obsolete; Vertex discontinued US Incivek sales in 2014. The episode became a defining lesson: a first-in-class asset can be a melting ice cube, and Vertex needed durable, hard-to-displace franchises.

### The cystic fibrosis franchise (2012–2019)
That durable franchise came from an unusual arrangement: the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation had funded Vertex's CFTR-modulator research since the late 1990s (venture philanthropy that the Foundation later monetized by selling its royalty stream to Royalty Pharma for $3.3 billion in 2014). Kalydeco (ivacaftor), approved January 2012, was the first drug to treat the underlying cause of CF, though only for ~4-5% of patients with gating mutations. Vertex then stacked combinations to reach the F508del majority: Orkambi (lumacaftor/ivacaftor, 2015), Symdeko/Symkevi (tezacaftor/ivacaftor, 2018), and the breakthrough triple combination Trikafta (elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor), approved in October 2019 five months ahead of its PDUFA date. Trikafta made highly effective modulator therapy available to roughly 90% of CF patients and drove revenue from ~$3B (2018) to over $11B (2024). Along the way Vertex fought bruising reimbursement battles — most visibly a multi-year standoff with England's NHS over Orkambi pricing, resolved in 2019. Jeffrey Leiden (CEO 2012–2020) moved headquarters to Boston's Seaport and handed the reins to Reshma Kewalramani in April 2020.

### Diversification: cell and genetic therapies (2015–2023)
With CF cash flowing, Vertex pursued a "serious diseases, causal biology" diversification strategy across modalities. A 2015 collaboration with CRISPR Therapeutics on gene editing produced exa-cel; in late 2023 Casgevy became the world's first approved CRISPR-based medicine (UK authorization November 2023; FDA approval for sickle cell disease December 2023, beta thalassemia January 2024). Vertex leads development, manufacturing and commercialization and books revenue, splitting costs and profits 60/40 with CRISPR Therapeutics. Acquisitions built the type 1 diabetes cell-therapy platform: Semma Therapeutics ($950M, 2019) and ViaCyte ($320M, 2022), yielding zimislecel (VX-880), a stem-cell-derived islet therapy. Exonics Therapeutics (2019, ~$245M upfront, DMD gene editing) was a smaller bet later deprioritized. Not everything worked: two alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency correctors (VX-814, VX-864) failed in 2020–2021, knocking the stock hard each time, and an encapsulated-islet program (VX-264) was discontinued in 2025.

### Pain, kidney disease and the post-Trikafta portfolio (2024–2025)
In April 2024 Vertex closed its largest-ever acquisition, Alpine Immune Sciences ($4.9B cash), for povetacicept, a dual BAFF/APRIL antagonist for IgA nephropathy. December 2024 brought a sharp stock drop when suzetrigine missed against placebo in a Phase 2 lumbosacral radiculopathy trial, but two landmark approvals followed within weeks of each other: Alyftrek (vanzacaftor/tezacaftor/deutivacaftor), a once-daily next-generation CF triple with a lower royalty burden, approved December 20, 2024, and Journavx (suzetrigine), approved January 30, 2025 — the first new class of pain medicine in over 20 years — an oral, non-opioid NaV1.8 inhibitor for moderate-to-severe acute pain, priced at $15.50 per pill. FY2025 closed at $12.0B revenue (+9%), with three simultaneous launches (Alyftrek, Journavx, Casgevy) beginning to diversify a business still overwhelmingly dependent on CF.

## Current strategic bets

Vertex's 2025–2026 story is executing three launches while pushing five-plus programs through pivotal development. Guidance for 2026 is $12.95–13.1B, with non-CF products expected to contribute $500M+.

**CF leadership defense.** Alyftrek is the succession plan for Trikafta: once-daily dosing, greater sweat-chloride reduction, label extensions (now ~95% of US CF patients eligible for a Vertex modulator after early-2026 expansions), pediatric age-group filings through 2026, and — importantly for margins — a lower royalty rate than Trikafta. Earlier-stage CF work includes VX-828 (next-gen corrector) and VX-522 (nebulized CFTR mRNA with Moderna, for patients who make no CFTR protein).

**Pain franchise.** Journavx's launch is deliberate and infrastructure-heavy (hospital formularies, group purchasing organizations); ~$33M in sales through Q3 2025 with over 300,000 prescriptions filled. The bigger prize is chronic/neuropathic pain: suzetrigine is in Phase 3 for painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy. A follow-on NaV1.8 inhibitor (VX-993) posted disappointing Phase 2 data in 2025.

**Kidney disease.** Povetacicept met its Phase 3 IgAN primary endpoint (proteinuria) and Vertex is filing for US accelerated approval in the first half of 2026 — potentially its next major launch, in a competitive APRIL/BAFF field (Otsuka, Vera, Novartis). Inaxaplin (APOL1-mediated kidney disease) is in the Phase 3 AMPLITUDE trial. Small-molecule polycystin correctors for ADPKD are earlier-stage.

**Type 1 diabetes.** Zimislecel, a one-time stem-cell-derived islet therapy, produced striking Phase 1/2 data (10 of 12 patients insulin-independent at one year; published in NEJM June 2025); Phase 3 dosing paused for a manufacturing analysis, then resumed in early 2026, with regulatory submissions targeted thereafter.

## Key risks

- **Extreme franchise concentration:** CF is still ~96% of FY2025 revenue (~$11.5B of $12.0B, est.). Any CF disruption — competitive (e.g., readthrough/mRNA entrants), pricing, or safety — hits the whole company. Trikafta-to-Alyftrek switching is also self-cannibalization that must be managed smoothly.
- **Patent cliffs:** Trikafta US composition-of-matter protection runs to ~2037 (est.) — distant but enormous (~$10B+ at risk); legacy products (Kalydeco, Orkambi, Symdeko) face LOE from ~2027 (est.), though they are already small. Alyftrek conversion is the hedge.
- **Launch execution risk:** Journavx uptake has trailed some analyst forecasts ($33M through Q3 2025 vs. Citi's $110M full-year projection); acute-pain reimbursement and hospital adoption are slow-build. Casgevy economics are long-cycle (complex ~12-month patient journey; 301 patient initiations in 2025).
- **Pipeline binary events:** povetacicept accelerated approval and confirmatory outcomes; inaxaplin Phase 3; zimislecel manufacturing/scale-up; suzetrigine in chronic pain after the 2024 LSR miss and 2025 VX-993 setback.
- **Pricing/policy:** IRA negotiation exposure is limited near-term (small-molecule CF drugs could eventually be selected; orphan-drug carve-outs help), but US net-price pressure, state Medicaid scrutiny of $300K+/yr list prices, and ex-US reimbursement fights (history with NHS England) recur. Political attention to drug pricing (2025 administration pressure) is a watch item.
- **Royalty obligations** to Royalty Pharma/CF Foundation on Trikafta-era CF drugs trim margins (one motivation for Alyftrek's lower royalty rate).
