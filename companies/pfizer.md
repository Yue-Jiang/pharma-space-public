## Pipeline (clinical-stage)

Pfizer has to replace roughly $20 billion of revenue that loses exclusivity by 2030, and it is buying two answers: obesity and more oncology. The obesity bet is PF-08653944, the ultra-long-acting injectable GLP-1 acquired with Metsera, now the subject of a ten-trial Phase 3 programme with a launch targeted for 2028. In cancer the Seagen-built antibody-drug-conjugate engine, a Chinese-licensed PD-1/VEGF bispecific and a run of next-generation breast-cancer drugs give Pfizer around twenty pivotal trial starts a year — the volume behind its claim of eight or more blockbuster oncology medicines by 2030.

## History: 1995-2025

### 1995-2000: Lipitor and the hostile bid that built modern Pfizer
In the late 1990s Pfizer transformed from a mid-sized pharma into the industry's revenue leader on the back of primary-care blockbusters. Viagra (sildenafil), approved in 1998, became a cultural phenomenon. More consequentially, Pfizer co-promoted Warner-Lambert's statin Lipitor (atorvastatin) — and when Warner-Lambert tried to merge with American Home Products in 1999, Pfizer launched a hostile counter-bid, closing a roughly $90 billion takeover in 2000 to capture full ownership. Lipitor became the best-selling drug in pharmaceutical history, peaking near $13 billion a year.

### 2000-2008: Scale as strategy, and its limits
Pfizer doubled down on megamergers, buying Pharmacia for about $60 billion in 2003 (adding Celebrex, Xalatan and an oncology base). But the decade exposed the weakness of acquisition-led growth: the COX-2 inhibitor Bextra (valdecoxib) was withdrawn in 2005 on safety grounds; torcetrapib, a $800M-plus cholesterol bet meant to succeed Lipitor, was halted in late 2006 when a Phase 3 trial showed increased deaths; and in 2009 Pfizer paid $2.3 billion to settle federal charges over off-label promotion of Bextra and other drugs — then the largest healthcare-fraud settlement in US history.

### 2009-2015: Wyeth, the Lipitor cliff, and slimming down
Facing Lipitor's 2011 US patent expiry — the largest patent cliff ever at the time — Pfizer bought Wyeth for $68 billion in 2009, gaining the Prevnar pneumococcal vaccine franchise and biologics capability. Post-Lipitor, revenue fell from ~$67B (2010) toward ~$49B (2016), and Pfizer shed non-core units: the nutrition business went to Nestlé for $11.85 billion (2012), and animal-health unit Zoetis was spun off (2013). Sterile-injectables maker Hospira was acquired for about $17 billion (2015).

### 2016-2019: Failed inversions, then a pivot to science
Two attempts to escape US tax rates via inversion failed: AstraZeneca rebuffed a ~$118 billion approach (2014), and the $160 billion Allergan merger was abandoned in April 2016 after new Treasury rules. Pfizer instead bought innovation — Medivation ($14B, 2016, adding Xtandi), Anacor ($5.2B, 2016) and Array Biopharma ($11.4B, 2019) — and under incoming CEO Albert Bourla (2019) split off its off-patent business Upjohn (merged with Mylan to form Viatris, 2020) and folded consumer health into a GSK joint venture that later listed as Haleon; Pfizer fully exited that stake in March 2025 for about $3.3 billion.

### 2020-2022: The COVID windfall
Pfizer's partnership with BioNTech produced Comirnaty, the first authorized COVID-19 vaccine (December 2020), followed by the oral antiviral Paxlovid (2021). Revenue exploded to a record $100.3 billion in 2022 — Comirnaty alone contributed ~$37.8B and Paxlovid ~$18.9B that year — making Pfizer briefly the largest pharma company by revenue in history.

### 2023-2025: The comedown and the rebuild
COVID demand collapsed; 2023 revenue fell to $58.5 billion with large Paxlovid write-offs, and the stock sank below pre-pandemic levels. Pfizer deployed its COVID cash into the biggest deal wave in its history — Arena ($6.7B), Biohaven ($11.6B) and Global Blood Therapeutics ($5.4B) in 2022, capped by Seagen, the antibody-drug-conjugate leader, for $43 billion (closed December 2023). Setbacks continued: sickle-cell drug Oxbryta (voxelotor), the core of the GBT deal, was withdrawn worldwide in 2024; the oral GLP-1 danuglipron was discontinued in April 2025 after a liver-injury case; and activist Starboard Value took a ~$1 billion stake in late 2024 criticizing capital allocation. A multi-year cost program delivered roughly $4.5 billion in net savings by end-2025 (targeting ~$5.7B by end-2026), and FY2025 closed at $62.6 billion in revenue — down 2%, but up 6% operationally excluding COVID products.

## Current strategic bets

Pfizer's 2025-2026 strategy is defined by three moves: buying its way back into obesity, doubling down on oncology, and being first to cut a pricing deal with the Trump administration.

**Obesity.** After danuglipron's failure, Pfizer won a public bidding war against Novo Nordisk for Metsera, closing in November 2025 at $65.60/share cash (~$7.0B enterprise value) plus contingent value rights worth up to ~$10B total. This brings MET-097i, a weekly/monthly injectable GLP-1 receptor agonist entering Phase 3, MET-233i, a monthly amylin analog in Phase 1 (mono and combination), and an oral GLP-1 in Phase 1. Management concedes the deal is earnings-dilutive through 2030 — a long-dated bet on the largest growth market in pharma.

**Oncology.** The Seagen-built ADC engine plus a stated goal of eight or more blockbuster oncology medicines by 2030. Key pipeline: sasanlimab (anti-PD-1, positive Phase 3 CREST in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer; EU registration phase), atirmociclib (selective CDK4 inhibitor, Phase 3, the planned Ibrance successor), sigvotatug vedotin (integrin beta-6 ADC, Phase 3 in NSCLC — the SigVie-002 second-line trial missed overall survival in June 2026, though first-line studies continue), Braftovi (encorafenib) expanding in BRAF-mutant colorectal cancer on strong BREAKWATER data, and Elrexfio moving into earlier myeloma lines. In May 2025 Pfizer paid a $1.25 billion upfront (plus up to $4.8B milestones and a $100M equity stake) to license SSGJ-707, a PD-1/VEGF bispecific from China's 3SBio — one of the largest China-originated licensing deals to date, with US manufacturing planned in North Carolina and Kansas.

**Policy positioning.** On September 30, 2025 Pfizer became the first pharma to sign a most-favored-nation pricing agreement with the Trump administration: MFN prices for Medicaid, discounted direct-to-consumer sales via TrumpRx.gov (live February 2026), MFN launch pricing for new drugs, and a pledged $70 billion in US R&D/manufacturing investment — in exchange for a three-year exemption from threatened 100% pharmaceutical tariffs.

Vaccines (Abrysvo expansion, next-generation pneumococcal candidates) and internal medicine round out the priorities. Guidance for 2026 is $59.5-62.5B revenue, absorbing ~$1.5B lower COVID revenue and ~$1.5B of LOE impact.

## Key risks

- **Patent cliff, 2026-2030:** the largest in the industry — Eliquis (~$8.0B FY2025, US generics ~2028), Vyndaqel family (~$6.4B, ~2028), Ibrance (~$4.1B, 2027), Xtandi (~$2.2B, 2027), Prevnar 13 and Xeljanz (2025-2026). Products representing roughly $20B+ of FY2025 revenue lose exclusivity by ~2030; 2026 alone carries ~$1.5B LOE impact per company guidance.
- **COVID franchise decay:** Comirnaty + Paxlovid fell to ~$6.7B combined in FY2025 (from ~$57B peak in 2022); 2026 guidance assumes another ~$1.5B decline; narrower US vaccine recommendations add policy risk.
- **IRA / pricing:** Eliquis Medicare-negotiated price effective January 2026; Ibrance and Xtandi selected for the 2027 round; Part D redesign was a ~$1B headwind in 2025. The MFN/TrumpRx agreement adds Medicaid pricing concessions with confidential terms.
- **Pipeline concentration & execution:** the obesity thesis rests on Metsera assets that are Phase 3-entry at best (after the danuglipron failure); oncology has had recent misses (SigVie-002 OS miss, Oxbryta withdrawal, disitamab vedotin deprioritized; $4.4B of Q4 2025 intangible impairments).
- **Litigation & other:** legacy Zantac claims (largely settled 2024-2025), COVID-vaccine-related suits and international contract disputes; heavy dividend commitment (~$9.7B/yr) with no buybacks constrains flexibility.
