## History: 1995-2025

### 2010–2019: Platform company, record checks, record IPO

Moderna was founded in Cambridge, Massachusetts in September 2010 as ModeRNA Therapeutics, built by Flagship Pioneering's Noubar Afeyan around stem-cell biologist Derrick Rossi's work with modified messenger RNA, alongside co-founders Robert Langer (MIT), Kenneth Chien and Timothy Springer. The core insight — chemically modified nucleosides let synthetic mRNA slip past innate immune sensors so cells can be instructed to make any protein — traced to Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman's academic work. Stéphane Bancel, recruited from bioMérieux in 2011, became CEO and has run the company ever since. Moderna raised roughly $2 billion in venture funding between 2011 and 2017, an extraordinary sum for a preclinical platform, and validated itself through partnerships: a 2013 AstraZeneca option deal with $240 million upfront (up to ~$420 million total) for cardiometabolic mRNA therapeutics, a 2014 Alexion rare-disease pact, DARPA funding, and a 2015–2016 alliance with Merck & Co. that would later matter enormously for oncology. Strategy drifted from secreted-protein therapeutics toward vaccines, where transient mRNA expression is a feature rather than a bug. In December 2018 the renamed Moderna went public on Nasdaq, raising about $604 million — then the largest biotech IPO in history — with a Norwood, Massachusetts manufacturing plant already running and no approved product.

### 2020–2022: The COVID windfall

In January 2020 Moderna designed mRNA-1273 against SARS-CoV-2 within days of the viral sequence posting, partnered with the US National Institutes of Health, and rode Operation Warp Speed funding through Phase 3. Emergency use authorization came on December 18, 2020 — the company's first authorized product, ten years after founding. Revenue exploded from $803 million in 2020 to $18.5 billion in 2021 (807 million doses delivered) and a peak $19.3 billion in 2022, with Spikevax (elasomeran) alone contributing about $18.4 billion and GAAP net income reaching $8.4 billion in 2022. Moderna ran multi-billion-dollar buybacks, banked over $13 billion in cash, and in 2022 sued Pfizer and BioNTech in multiple jurisdictions, claiming Comirnaty infringed its modified-uridine patents — after narrowing an earlier pandemic-era pledge not to enforce them.

### 2023–2024: The bust

The endemic transition was brutal. Revenue fell to $6.8 billion in 2023 and $3.2 billion in 2024, with GAAP net losses of $4.7 billion and $3.6 billion respectively, as COVID vaccination collapsed to a seasonal, largely US commercial market where Pfizer held share. The diversification bets partially delivered: mRESVIA, the RSV vaccine, won FDA approval in May 2024 — Moderna's second product and the first mRNA vaccine approved for anything other than COVID — but launched late into a market GSK's Arexvy and Pfizer's Abrysvo had already claimed, booking just $25 million in 2024. The Merck-partnered individualized neoantigen therapy mRNA-4157 posted a 44–49% reduction in recurrence risk in adjuvant melanoma (KEYNOTE-942), triggering Phase 3 trials. In September 2024 Moderna announced a $1.5 billion opex reduction plan through 2027, initially without layoffs.

### 2025: Reset, politics and litigation

2025 revenue fell further to $1.9 billion ($678 million in Q4), with a $2.8 billion net loss — cushioned by cost cuts that took GAAP operating expenses down $2.2 billion (30%) year over year, and $8.1 billion in year-end cash. The company laid off about 10% of staff in July 2025, taking headcount below 5,000. The political environment turned hostile: in May 2025, HHS under Robert F. Kennedy Jr. canceled a ~$590 million BARDA contract for Moderna's H5N1 avian-flu vaccine, calling mRNA "under-tested," despite positive Phase 1/2 data. mNEXSPIKE (next-generation COVID vaccine) won FDA approval in May 2025 and quickly captured about a quarter of US retail COVID vaccination. Setbacks continued: the cytomegalovirus vaccine mRNA-1647 — once billed as a future blockbuster — failed its Phase 3 CMVictory trial in October 2025 (6–23% efficacy) and was discontinued for congenital CMV. Litigation cut both ways: the UK Court of Appeal upheld Moderna's key patent against Pfizer/BioNTech in August 2025 (a German court ruled similarly in March 2025, though the US PTAB invalidated two Moderna patents), while in March 2026 Moderna settled the long-running Arbutus/Genevant LNP infringement case for $950 million upfront plus up to $1.3 billion contingent on appeal — potentially the largest pharma patent settlement ever. In February 2026 the FDA issued a refusal-to-file for the flu vaccine mRNA-1010; after a Type A meeting the application was accepted and mFLUSIVA was approved on August 5, 2026 for adults 50+, Moderna's fourth US product. CureVac sued Moderna over Spikevax in April 2026.

## Current strategic bets

Moderna's strategy for 2025–2026 is a seasonal respiratory-vaccine franchise funding an oncology and rare-disease pipeline, under severe cost discipline: guidance targets up to 10% revenue growth in 2026, opex down to $4.7–5.0 billion by 2027, and cash breakeven by 2028. Growth levers are geographic (EU launches of mNEXSPIKE and mCOMBRIAX from 2026–27, multi-year supply agreements with Mexico and Taiwan), new launches (mFLUSIVA in the US for 2026–27), and eventually norovirus — though mRNA-1403 missed its Phase 3 interim early-success criteria (mid-2026) and is enrolling an additional cohort.

The flagship pipeline bet is intismeran autogene (mRNA-4157/V940), the Merck-partnered individualized neoantigen therapy: five-year KEYNOTE-942 follow-up (January 2026) showed a sustained 49% reduction in recurrence or death in resected high-risk melanoma, with eight to nine Phase 2/3 trials running across melanoma, non-small cell lung, bladder and renal cancers; Phase 3 melanoma (INTerpath-001) is fully enrolled with interim data possible in 2026. In rare disease, Moderna licensed global commercialization of its propionic acidemia candidate to Recordati (February 2026). No significant China-originated in-licensing — Moderna is an mRNA pure-play built on its own platform.

## Key risks

- **Revenue concentration in COVID**: $1.81B of $1.82B FY2025 product sales was the COVID franchise; the market shrinks each season and Pfizer/BioNTech dominate share. mRESVIA has effectively failed commercially ($8M in 2025).
- **US policy/regulatory hostility to mRNA**: HHS canceled the $590M H5N1 contract (May 2025); the flu refusal-to-file (Feb 2026) showed unpredictable FDA behavior even though it was ultimately reversed; ACIP/CDC vaccine-policy turmoil threatens US uptake and reimbursement.
- **Cash burn and litigation payouts**: net loss of $2.8B in 2025; the Arbutus/Genevant settlement costs $950M (Q3 2026) plus up to $1.3B contingent; year-end 2026 cash guided to $4.5–5.0B; breakeven not expected before 2028.
- **Pipeline binary risk**: the equity story leans heavily on intismeran autogene Phase 3 readouts; CMV's failure (Oct 2025) and the norovirus interim miss (2026) show platform breadth does not guarantee wins.
- **Ongoing IP disputes**: offensive cases against Pfizer/BioNTech are before the UK Supreme Court; CureVac filed a new US suit (April 2026).
- Patent cliffs in the classic sense are not the issue — demand, policy and competition are.
