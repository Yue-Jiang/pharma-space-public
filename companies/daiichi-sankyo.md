## History: 1995-2025

### Two legacies and a merger (pre-2005–2007)
Daiichi Sankyo's two parent companies each carried more than a century of history. Sankyo, founded in 1899, commercialized Jokichi Takamine's adrenaline and later became the birthplace of the statin class: Akira Endo discovered compactin at Sankyo in the 1970s, and Sankyo's pravastatin (Mevalotin) became one of Japan's biggest drugs. Daiichi Pharmaceutical, founded in 1915, discovered the antibiotic levofloxacin (Cravit/Levaquin). The two merged in 2005 under a joint holding company, completing full integration in 2007, creating Japan's then-second-largest pharma with a heavily cardiovascular-metabolic portfolio anchored by the hypertension blockbuster Benicar (olmesartan), which at its peak contributed roughly a quarter of revenue.

### The Ranbaxy misadventure (2008–2015)
In June 2008 Daiichi Sankyo paid $4.6 billion for a controlling (~64%) stake in India's Ranbaxy Laboratories — the largest acquisition in Indian pharma history — betting on a "hybrid" innovator-plus-generics model. Within months the FDA barred imports from key Ranbaxy plants over data-integrity failures. Ranbaxy pleaded guilty in 2013 and paid a record $500 million in US fines. In April 2014 Daiichi Sankyo cut its losses, selling Ranbaxy to Sun Pharmaceutical in an all-stock deal (~$3.2 billion plus debt), then divested its resulting ~9% Sun stake for about $3.6 billion in 2015. A Singapore arbitration panel in 2016 awarded Daiichi Sankyo $525 million from Ranbaxy's former Singh-family shareholders for concealing the severity of the FDA problems during the sale. The episode is a standard case study in emerging-market M&A diligence failure.

### Patent cliff and quiet oncology seeding (2011–2017)
While digesting Ranbaxy, the company faced the loss of Benicar exclusivity (US LOE 2016) and restructured, cutting staff in Japan and the US. Litigation followed the franchise: a $39 million US kickback settlement (2015) and a $300 million products-liability settlement (2017, later ~$358 million) over olmesartan-linked sprue-like enteropathy. Meanwhile, management seeded oncology through bolt-on M&A: U3 Pharma (2008, €150 million; source of the HER3 antibody patritumab), Plexxikon (2011, $805 million upfront; vemurafenib and pexidartinib), and Ambit Biosciences (2014, up to $410 million; quizartinib). Edoxaban (Lixiana/Savaysa), approved in 2011 (Japan) and 2015 (US/EU), grew into the company's cardiovascular successor franchise — though it never displaced Eliquis or Xarelto in the US.

### DXd and the ADC transformation (2017–2020)
The pivot that redefined the company came from in-house chemistry: the DXd antibody-drug conjugate platform, pairing antibodies with a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload (an exatecan derivative) via a cleavable tetrapeptide linker with a high drug-to-antibody ratio. Striking phase 1 data for DS-8201 (trastuzumab deruxtecan) in 2017 changed the company's trajectory. In March 2019 AstraZeneca paid $1.35 billion upfront in a deal worth up to $6.9 billion to co-develop and co-commercialize what became Enhertu, sharing costs and profits globally except Japan. Enhertu won accelerated US approval in December 2019 for third-line HER2-positive metastatic breast cancer. In July 2020 AstraZeneca doubled down with a second deal — up to $6 billion, $1 billion upfront — for the TROP2 ADC datopotamab deruxtecan.

### Enhertu ascendant (2020–2023)
DESTINY-Breast03 showed unprecedented superiority over the prior standard T-DM1; DESTINY-Breast04 (2022) established the new "HER2-low" treatment category, and approvals accumulated in gastric cancer, HER2-mutant lung cancer, and later the first tumor-agnostic HER2 approval. Combined Enhertu sales reached $2.6 billion in 2023 and $3.75 billion in 2024. Smaller oncology launches added depth: Turalio (2019), Ezharmia (Japan, 2022), Vanflyta (US, 2023). In October 2023 Merck & Co. validated the platform again, paying $4 billion upfront plus $1.5 billion in continuation payments — up to $22 billion with sales milestones — for three more DXd ADCs: patritumab deruxtecan (HER3), ifinatamab deruxtecan (B7-H3) and raludotatug deruxtecan (CDH6), co-commercialized worldwide except Japan.

### Growing pains and a legal vindication (2024–2026)
Not everything worked. Patritumab deruxtecan received a manufacturing-related complete response letter in June 2024, and in May 2025 the US application was withdrawn entirely after the confirmatory HERTHENA-Lung02 trial missed statistical significance on overall survival. Datopotamab deruxtecan's initial lung strategy was scaled back, though Datroway won US approvals in HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer (January 2025), EGFR-mutated lung cancer (2025) and first-line triple-negative breast cancer (May 2026). The long-running patent war with Seagen (later Pfizer) over ADC linker technology ended in Daiichi Sankyo's favor: a 2022 Texas jury verdict of $41.8 million plus royalties was reversed by the Federal Circuit in December 2025 after the patent was held invalid. Enhertu kept climbing — first-line metastatic HER2-positive breast approval in December 2025, then neoadjuvant/adjuvant early breast cancer approvals in May 2026. FY2025 revenue reached ¥2.12 trillion, though Q4 absorbed ¥133 billion in one-time charges (including a ¥75.7 billion CMO compensation payment).

## Current strategic bets

The five-year business plan announced May 2026 targets revenue above ¥3 trillion and operating profit above ¥600 billion by FY2030, and "Global Top 5 Oncology Company" status by 2035 — with a stated goal of 20 new indication launches across five oncology medicines by 2030. The strategy remains a concentrated bet on the DXd ADC platform, monetized through the two mega-alliances (AstraZeneca for Enhertu/Datroway; Merck for the next three ADCs), with Daiichi Sankyo keeping manufacturing, Japan rights, and roughly half the economics. Enhertu's push into early-stage breast cancer and first-line settings is the main near-term revenue engine; a gastric-cancer overall-survival win (2026) adds another expansion.

Management under Okuzawa is deliberately platform-centric rather than BD-heavy: unlike most large-cap peers in 2024–2026, Daiichi Sankyo has not made major China-originated in-licensing deals, relying instead on in-house discovery (next-generation ADC formats and payloads) plus research collaborations (e.g., BostonGene on AI multimodal analytics, 2026). Jefferies and other analysts have flagged this as a risk as rivals crowd into ADCs with cheaper China-sourced assets. Non-oncology diversification is modest: Tarlige and Lixiana in Japan/Asia, Nilemdo momentum in Europe, and a vaccines business in Japan (including an mRNA COVID-19 vaccine, Daichirona).

## Key risks

- **Lixiana LOE**: ~¥350B (est.) franchise faces US patent expiry ~2028 and Europe ~2030, with Japanese price cuts already biting — the single largest revenue at risk before 2030.
- **Concentration**: Enhertu alone is roughly a third of revenue (including alliance income); the whole equity story rests on one payload-linker platform. Any DXd class safety issue (interstitial lung disease is a known class effect) or ADC competitive leapfrog (bispecific ADCs, China-originated assets) hits everything at once.
- **Shared economics**: Half of Enhertu/Datroway ex-Japan profits go to AstraZeneca and future ADC profits to Merck; headline "combined sales" overstate what Daiichi Sankyo keeps.
- **Pipeline execution**: patritumab deruxtecan's 2025 withdrawal and Datroway's lung-cancer strategy change show confirmatory-trial risk; Jefferies (2026) argues the next-gen pipeline lags rivals as Enhertu/Datroway approach peak.
- **IRA / pricing**: Enhertu's expanding US Medicare exposure makes it a plausible IRA negotiation candidate in the early 2030s (est.); Japan repricing pressure is chronic.
- **Enhertu LOE ~2033 (US, est.)**: the company must build its next franchise before its crown jewel expires.
- **Litigation/one-offs largely resolved**: Seagen verdict reversed (Dec 2025); Benicar and Ranbaxy matters closed; FY2025 took a ¥75.7B CMO compensation charge.
