Today's deep dive: Novartis, the Basel-based Swiss giant that has spent a decade shedding businesses to become what it calls a pure-play innovative medicines company.

Start with the numbers. In full-year twenty twenty-five, Novartis booked about fifty-four and a half billion dollars in net sales, up eight percent at constant currencies, and for the first time hit a forty percent core operating margin, with core operating income up twelve percent. The chief executive is Vas Narasimhan, a physician who has run the company since twenty eighteen, and the board is chaired by Giovanni Caforio, the former Bristol Myers Squibb chief executive. Novartis employs roughly seventy-six to seventy-eight thousand people and focuses on four therapeutic areas: cardiovascular, renal and metabolic disease; immunology; neuroscience; and oncology — plus technology platforms in radioligand therapy, gene therapy, and R N A medicines.

Novartis was born in December nineteen ninety-six from the merger of two Basel neighbors, Ciba-Geigy and Sandoz — a roughly thirty billion dollar deal that was, at the time, the largest corporate merger in history. The name comes from the Latin for "new skills." The original company was a conglomerate spanning drugs, generics, agrochemicals and nutrition, and its history since is essentially one long exercise in focus. In two thousand, the agribusiness was spun off and merged with AstraZeneca's crop science arm to form Syngenta.

The defining scientific moment came in two thousand one, when the F D A approved Gleevec, generic name imatinib. Gleevec turned chronic myeloid leukemia from a death sentence into a manageable chronic disease, became the poster child for targeted cancer therapy, and grew into a multi-billion dollar franchise. Alongside it, the blood-pressure pill Diovan, generic name valsartan, peaked at around six billion dollars a year. The two thousands were also the empire-building years: Novartis assembled a generics powerhouse under the revived Sandoz name, bought the vaccines maker Chiron, and between two thousand eight and twenty eleven spent roughly fifty-two billion dollars taking over the eye-care company Alcon from Nestlé.

Then came the pruning. After Diovan lost United States exclusivity in twenty twelve, Novartis executed a famous asset swap in twenty fourteen and twenty fifteen: it bought GlaxoSmithKline's cancer drug portfolio for about sixteen billion dollars, sold its vaccines business to GlaxoSmithKline, pushed consumer health into a joint venture with the same company, and sold animal health to Eli Lilly. Twenty fifteen brought two launches that would carry the next decade: Entresto, generic name sacubitril valsartan, for heart failure, and Cosentyx, generic name secukinumab, an interleukin seventeen antibody for psoriasis and related conditions.

When Vas Narasimhan took over in twenty eighteen, he finished the job of dismantling the conglomerate. The consumer joint-venture stake was sold to GlaxoSmithKline for thirteen billion dollars. Alcon was spun off in twenty nineteen. The long-held voting stake in rival Roche was sold back for nearly twenty-one billion dollars in twenty twenty-one. And in October twenty twenty-three, Sandoz itself — the generics business carrying one of the two founding names — was spun off as an independent company.

At the same time, Narasimhan bought technology platforms. The eight point seven billion dollar AveXis deal in twenty eighteen brought Zolgensma, a one-time gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy that launched at two point one million dollars per dose — then the most expensive drug in the world — and promptly landed in a data-manipulation scandal over animal studies. The Endocyte acquisition brought radioligand therapy, which delivered Pluvicto, a radioactive drug that seeks out prostate cancer cells. The nearly ten billion dollar purchase of The Medicines Company brought Leqvio, generic name inclisiran, a twice-yearly cholesterol-lowering injection based on gene-silencing technology licensed from Alnylam. There were stumbles too: in twenty twenty Novartis paid six hundred seventy-eight million dollars to settle allegations of sham speaker programs for doctors, and three hundred forty-seven million dollars over foreign bribery charges.

So where does the company stand today? The twenty twenty-five portfolio tells a story of handover. Entresto, still the biggest product at seven point seven billion dollars, lost United States exclusivity in twenty twenty-five, and generics are eroding it fast. Blood-platelet drug Promacta and leukemia drug Tasigna went generic the same year. Novartis itself calls twenty twenty-six the largest patent expiry year in its history. But the next generation is scaling quickly. Cosentyx did six point seven billion. The breast cancer pill Kisqali, generic name ribociclib, surged fifty-seven percent to four point eight billion after strong data in early breast cancer, and analysts now discuss a peak-sales ambition of around ten billion dollars — an estimate, not a confirmed company target. Kesimpta, a self-injected multiple sclerosis antibody, reached four point four billion. Pluvicto hit two billion. Scemblix, a novel leukemia pill, and Leqvio each crossed one billion. And Fabhalta, an oral complement inhibitor for rare blood and kidney diseases, is ramping.

Strategically, Novartis is spending through the cliff. Twenty twenty-five brought seventeen deals in total: Anthos Therapeutics, nine hundred twenty-five million dollars upfront and up to three point one billion with milestones, bringing back a blood-clot-prevention antibody that originated in Novartis's own labs; Regulus Therapeutics, eight hundred million dollars plus contingent payments, for a polycystic kidney disease drug; Tourmaline Bio, about one point four billion, for an anti-inflammatory heart drug; and the headline deal — roughly twelve billion dollars for Avidity Biosciences, whose antibody-linked R N A drugs reach muscle tissue, targeting Duchenne muscular dystrophy, myotonic dystrophy and related diseases. Novartis also pledged twenty-three billion dollars for United States manufacturing and research amid tariff and drug-pricing politics.

The pipeline now faces a run of make-or-break readouts. Pelacarsen, partnered with Ionis, is the first drug designed to lower lipoprotein little-a, a genetic cardiovascular risk factor with no existing treatment — its outcomes trial is the readout analysts watch most. Rhapsido, generic name remibrutinib, an oral B T K inhibitor approved in twenty twenty-five for chronic hives, is being tested in multiple sclerosis, where a positive readout is widely seen as the key near-term growth question for the company. Abelacimab could offer blood-thinning with less bleeding risk. Ianalumab is heading for a launch in Sjögren's disease. And the Avidity drugs are expected to launch before twenty thirty.

The risk list is real. About ten billion dollars of revenue sits exposed to generics through twenty twenty-seven. The United States Inflation Reduction Act put Entresto in the first round of Medicare price negotiation. The growth case leans heavily on Kisqali, which itself loses protection in the early twenty thirties. And the twenty twenty-five shopping spree has to convert into approved, reimbursed launches on schedule.

Still, the through-line of Novartis history is remarkable consistency of intent: from sprawling conglomerate to focused medicines company, funding each patent cliff with the proceeds of the last divestiture and a bet on new modalities. Guidance calls for five to six percent annual sales growth through twenty thirty. Whether radioligands, gene silencing and muscle-targeted R N A can outrun the generics — that is the Novartis story to watch.
