Vertex Pharmaceuticals is the biotech industry's best example of what happens when a company wins a disease outright. Headquartered in Boston, Massachusetts, Vertex booked about twelve billion dollars of revenue in twenty twenty-five, employs roughly six thousand four hundred people, and is led by chief executive Reshma Kewalramani. Nearly all of that money comes from a single condition: cystic fibrosis, a genetic disease that clogs the lungs and digestive system with thick mucus. But the more interesting story is what Vertex is doing with that cash: gene editing, a new class of painkiller, and attempts at functional cures for type one diabetes and serious kidney diseases.

Start at the beginning. Vertex was founded in nineteen eighty-nine by Joshua Boger, a chemist who left the pharmaceutical giant Merck to prove that drugs could be designed rationally, atom by atom, around the structure of a target protein, rather than discovered by brute-force screening. The company's chaotic early years were chronicled in a well-known book called The Billion-Dollar Molecule. For two decades Vertex was a brilliant research shop that lost money. Its first marketed successes were human immunodeficiency virus medicines partnered with GlaxoSmithKline in the late nineties and early two-thousands.

The first transformation came in twenty eleven with a hepatitis C drug called telaprevir, sold as Incivek. It was one of the fastest drug launches in history, briefly earning well over a billion dollars a year. Then it collapsed almost overnight, when Gilead Sciences introduced a far better hepatitis C regimen. By twenty fourteen Incivek was gone. The lesson burned into Vertex's culture: being first is not enough. You need medicines so good, in diseases so well understood, that nobody can displace you.

Cystic fibrosis became exactly that. In an unusual arrangement, the Cystic Fibrosis Foundation, a patient charity, had funded Vertex's research for years, a bet so successful that the foundation later sold its royalty rights for three point three billion dollars. The first drug, Kalydeco, approved in twenty twelve, was the first medicine ever to treat the root cause of cystic fibrosis rather than its symptoms, but it only worked for about five percent of patients. Vertex then spent the decade stacking corrector molecules into combinations that could help the majority: Orkambi in twenty fifteen, Symdeko in twenty eighteen, and then the breakthrough, Trikafta, a triple combination approved in October twenty nineteen, five months ahead of schedule. Trikafta made highly effective modulator therapy available to roughly ninety percent of people with cystic fibrosis. Revenue rose from about three billion dollars in twenty eighteen to more than eleven billion in twenty twenty-four, and Trikafta alone now brings in over ten billion dollars a year.

That dominance is also Vertex's biggest vulnerability: about ninety-six percent of revenue still comes from cystic fibrosis. So the company has been spending aggressively to diversify, and twenty twenty-five was the year the strategy became visible, with three product launches running at once.

First, gene editing. Back in twenty fifteen, Vertex partnered with CRISPR Therapeutics, whose name refers to clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats. In late twenty twenty-three their therapy Casgevy became the first medicine based on that gene-editing system to win approval anywhere. Casgevy is a one-time treatment for severe sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent beta thalassemia: a patient's own blood stem cells are removed, edited to produce fetal hemoglobin, and infused back. Vertex leads the program and splits economics sixty-forty with its partner. The launch is slow by design because the patient journey takes about a year. Casgevy generated about one hundred sixteen million dollars in twenty twenty-five sales, with three hundred and one patient initiations during the year.

Second, cystic fibrosis succession. In December twenty twenty-four the Food and Drug Administration approved Alyftrek, a once-daily next-generation triple combination that produced greater reductions in sweat chloride and carries a lower royalty burden than Trikafta. Moving patients from Trikafta to Alyftrek protects the franchise into the late twenty thirties and beyond, since Trikafta's key patents run to around twenty thirty-seven.

Third, and most watched: pain. Weeks after Alyftrek, in January twenty twenty-five, the Food and Drug Administration approved Journavx, generic name suzetrigine, the first genuinely new class of pain medicine in more than twenty years. It blocks a sodium channel found only in peripheral pain-sensing nerves, which means it relieves moderate-to-severe acute pain without opioid-style addiction risk. The launch has been a slow build, about thirty-three million dollars through the first three quarters of twenty twenty-five and short of some analyst hopes, because hospital formularies and insurance coverage take time. But over three hundred thousand prescriptions were filled in year one, and Vertex is running a Phase Three trial of the same molecule in painful diabetic peripheral neuropathy, a much larger chronic market.

The pipeline behind all this is deep. In twenty twenty-four Vertex made its largest acquisition ever at the time, paying four point nine billion dollars for Alpine Immune Sciences to get povetacicept, a protein drug for immunoglobulin A nephropathy, a progressive autoimmune kidney disease. Povetacicept met its primary goal in Phase Three and now faces a Food and Drug Administration decision on November thirtieth, twenty twenty-six, under accelerated approval. There is also inaxaplin, in Phase Three for a genetically defined kidney disease driven by apolipoprotein L one gene variants; and zimislecel, a one-time stem-cell-derived islet cell therapy for severe type one diabetes. In a small early study, ten of twelve zimislecel patients no longer needed insulin injections a year after treatment, according to data published in the New England Journal of Medicine. Earlier acquisitions of Semma Therapeutics and ViaCyte built that diabetes platform.

Vertex raised the acquisition stakes again in July twenty twenty-six by agreeing to pay about ten billion dollars for Crinetics Pharmaceuticals. The deal adds Palsonify, generic name paltusotine, an oral treatment already approved for acromegaly, and atumelnant, a Phase Three oral drug for congenital adrenal hyperplasia. Vertex estimates those two endocrine assets could together exceed five billion dollars in peak annual sales. The purchase would give Vertex a commercial rare-endocrine franchise while the kidney, pain, and diabetes programs mature.

The record includes real failures, and Vertex is willing to kill programs. Two drug candidates for alpha-one antitrypsin deficiency failed in twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one. An encapsulated islet-cell approach for diabetes was dropped in twenty twenty-five. A follow-on pain molecule disappointed in mid-stage trials, and suzetrigine itself missed against placebo in one chronic back-and-leg-pain study in late twenty twenty-four, causing a sharp stock drop.

What are the risks? Concentration, above all: cystic fibrosis pays for everything, and the company must convert its own patients to Alyftrek without a stumble. Launch execution: Journavx and Casgevy are both infrastructure-heavy launches that could underwhelm. Pipeline binaries: povetacicept's approval decision, inaxaplin's Phase Three readout, and zimislecel's manufacturing scale-up are each significant swing factors. The Crinetics deal adds integration and capital-allocation risk. And pricing remains a pressure point: Vertex's medicines list at around three hundred thousand dollars a year in cystic fibrosis, which draws recurring reimbursement fights. The company famously spent years in a standoff with England's National Health Service, and political attention to drug prices in the United States continues.

Still, the numbers going forward are enviable: guidance for twenty twenty-six is around thirteen billion dollars, with non-cystic-fibrosis products expected to pass half a billion for the first time. Vertex's bet is that the playbook that conquered cystic fibrosis, picking serious diseases with clear causal biology, going after the root cause, pricing high, and defending hard, can work again in pain, kidney disease, blood disorders, diabetes, and endocrine disease. The next two years, with povetacicept's approval decision and zimislecel's filing, will show whether that bet pays off.
