Today's deep dive is Sanofi, the French pharmaceutical giant headquartered in Paris. In twenty twenty-five, Sanofi booked about forty-three point six billion euros in sales, up almost ten percent at constant exchange rates, making it one of the world's largest drugmakers. It employs roughly seventy-five thousand people. And it is a company in the middle of two dramas at once: a spectacular dependence on a single medicine, and a sudden change at the very top.

Let's start with where the company came from, because Sanofi is really the story of French industrial consolidation. Sanofi itself was founded in nineteen seventy-three as a subsidiary of the oil company Elf Aquitaine. In nineteen ninety-nine it merged with Synthélabo, a drugmaker that had grown up under the cosmetics group L'Oréal. That same year, on the other side of the Rhine, Germany's Hoechst and France's Rhône-Poulenc merged to form Aventis. Then in two thousand four came the defining deal: Sanofi-Synthélabo acquired the much larger Aventis for roughly fifty-five billion euros, with the French government quietly encouraging a domestic marriage rather than letting Swiss rival Novartis swoop in. The combined sanofi-aventis was, at the time, the third largest drug company on earth. The name was simplified to just Sanofi in twenty eleven.

Through the two thousands, Sanofi lived off a portfolio of primary care blockbusters: the blood thinner Plavix, known generically as clopidogrel; the injectable anticoagulant Lovenox; the sleep drug Ambien; the allergy pill Allegra; and above all Lantus, the long-acting insulin that became its biggest seller. Then the patents ran out. Plavix lost United States exclusivity in twenty twelve, and Lantus followed in twenty fifteen. Chief executive Chris Viehbacher answered the first cliff by diversifying: consumer health, animal health, and most importantly the twenty point one billion dollar acquisition of Genzyme in twenty eleven, which made Sanofi a world leader in rare diseases like Gaucher disease, Fabry disease, and Pompe disease. Viehbacher was abruptly fired in twenty fourteen; his successor Olivier Brandicourt endured insulin pricing wars, a commercial disappointment with the cholesterol antibody Praluent, and a genuine crisis in twenty seventeen, when the Philippines halted a school vaccination program using Sanofi's dengue vaccine, Dengvaxia, after data showed risks in children who had never been infected.

But the Brandicourt years also delivered the medicine that now defines the company. Dupixent, generic name dupilumab, was developed with the American biotech Regeneron and approved in twenty seventeen for atopic dermatitis, the severe form of eczema. It works by blocking a receptor for two immune signaling molecules, interleukin four and interleukin thirteen, which drive allergic-type inflammation. Indication after indication followed: asthma, chronic sinus disease with nasal polyps, a swallowing disorder called eosinophilic esophagitis, chronic hives, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and more. In twenty twenty-five, Dupixent brought in about seventeen point eight billion dollars globally — roughly a third of all Sanofi sales, and in early twenty twenty-six it touched forty percent of quarterly revenue. It is one of the best-selling medicines in the world.

In September twenty nineteen, Paul Hudson arrived as chief executive with a strategy he called Play to Win. He made hard choices fast: Sanofi walked away from diabetes and cardiovascular research — a symbolic break for the house of Lantus — and poured money into immunology, vaccines, and rare diseases. He went shopping: Principia brought two promising oral drugs that block an enzyme called Bruton's tyrosine kinase; Kadmon brought a graft-versus-host disease drug; Translate Bio brought messenger R N A technology; Provention Bio brought Tzield, the first drug to delay the onset of type one diabetes; and Kymab brought an antibody called amlitelimab that may one day be Dupixent's heir. There were stumbles too. Sanofi, one of the world's great vaccine makers, was late with its Covid vaccine. And in October twenty twenty-three, Hudson stunned investors by scrapping a profit margin target to spend more on research — the stock fell nearly a fifth in a day.

Two newer products then changed the mood. Beyfortus, generic name nirsevimab, developed with AstraZeneca, is a long-acting antibody given to newborns to prevent respiratory syncytial virus, the leading cause of infant hospitalization. It reached about one point eight billion euros in twenty twenty-five sales. Altuviiio, a once-weekly engineered factor eight therapy for hemophilia A, crossed one point one billion euros in its second full year. The rare disease engine kept humming, and the multiple myeloma antibody Sarclisa and the graft-versus-host drug Rezurock kept growing.

Twenty twenty-five was the year Sanofi finished remaking itself. In April it closed the sale of a fifty percent controlling stake in Opella, its consumer healthcare arm — the maker of Allegra and Doliprane — to the private equity firm Clayton, Dubilier and Rice, at an enterprise value of about sixteen billion euros. Sanofi pocketed roughly ten billion euros in cash, kept a forty-eight percent stake, and declared itself a pure-play biopharma company. It immediately redeployed the money: nine point one billion dollars upfront for Blueprint Medicines, whose drug Ayvakit is the only approved therapy for a mast cell disorder called systemic mastocytosis; around half a billion for Vigil Neuroscience in Alzheimer's disease; over a billion for the vaccine developer Vicebio; a one point seven billion dollar licensing deal with Earendil Labs for antibodies discovered by artificial intelligence; and, in early twenty twenty-six, a one and a half billion dollar global license for rovadicitinib, an oral drug from China's Sino Biopharm — part of a broader industry wave of China-originated deals.

There were real wins. Wayrilz, generic name rilzabrutinib, became the first Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitor approved for immune thrombocytopenia, a bleeding disorder — cleared in the United States in August twenty twenty-five, then Europe and Japan. Amlitelimab, the potential Dupixent successor, delivered positive phase three results in atopic dermatitis. But the biggest neurology bet wobbled badly. Tolebrutinib, an oral drug designed to reach the brain and quiet smoldering inflammation in multiple sclerosis, failed in primary progressive disease, and then, despite breakthrough status and strong data in non-relapsing secondary progressive multiple sclerosis, the Food and Drug Administration rejected it in December twenty twenty-five with a complete response letter — a decision Sanofi publicly called a significant and disappointing change of direction.

The board's patience ran out. In February twenty twenty-six, Sanofi ousted Paul Hudson and recruited Belén Garijo, the Spanish physician who had been running Germany's Merck — and who, years earlier, had led the Genzyme integration inside Sanofi itself. She took over at the end of April twenty twenty-six as the first woman to lead the company, with a mandate for research productivity and disciplined execution.

So what should you watch? First, the cliff: Dupixent's key United States patent expires in March twenty thirty-one, and no single pipeline asset can plausibly replace fifteen billion euros of annual revenue on its own. Second, the vaccine business, which faces vaccine skepticism and policy turbulence in the United States — Sanofi guided vaccine sales slightly down for twenty twenty-six. Third, pricing: insulins are already subject to Medicare negotiation, and Sanofi signed on to a broader agreement with the American government to lower medicine prices. Fourth, the tail of the Zantac litigation, mostly settled for a few hundred million dollars but not entirely gone. And finally, whether amlitelimab, rilzabrutinib, the Blueprint portfolio, and a rebuilt neurology franchise can together fill the twenty-thirties gap.

Sanofi today is a leaner, more focused company than at any point in its history — one great drug, a genuinely improving launch engine, a war chest, and a new leader hired to make the science pay. The next five years decide whether the pure-play bet was the right one.
