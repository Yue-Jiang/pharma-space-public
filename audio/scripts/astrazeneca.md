AstraZeneca is the Anglo-Swedish giant headquartered in Cambridge, England, a company that fell off one of the steepest patent cliffs in pharmaceutical history, nearly got swallowed by Pfizer, and then engineered one of the great turnarounds in the industry. In twenty twenty-five it brought in about fifty-nine billion dollars in revenue, up nine percent, with roughly ninety-five thousand employees worldwide. Its chief executive, Pascal Soriot, a French veterinarian by training who came over from Roche, has run the company since October twenty twelve, making him one of the longest-serving bosses in big pharma.

The company was born in April nineteen ninety-nine from the merger of Astra, a Swedish firm founded in nineteen thirteen, and Zeneca, the pharmaceutical business that had been spun out of Britain's Imperial Chemical Industries in nineteen ninety-three. At the time, Astra owned the world's best-selling drug: the stomach-acid blocker Losec, known generically as omeprazole. Zeneca brought cancer medicines like tamoxifen. The combined company spent its first decade riding enormous primary-care franchises: Nexium for reflux, Crestor for cholesterol, Seroquel for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, and the Symbicort inhaler for asthma. There were stumbles too, the lung cancer pill Iressa disappointed in broad populations before anyone understood it only worked in patients with certain genetic mutations, and in twenty ten the company paid five hundred twenty million dollars to settle claims it had marketed Seroquel off-label. The most consequential move of that era was the roughly fifteen billion dollar purchase of the biotech company MedImmune in two thousand seven. Analysts called it overpriced, but it gave AstraZeneca the antibody engine that later produced several of today's blockbusters.

Then came the cliff. Between twenty twelve and twenty sixteen, Seroquel, Nexium and Crestor all lost patent protection, and revenue collapsed from about thirty-three billion dollars to around twenty-three billion. Into that crisis walked Pascal Soriot, who bet everything on oncology. In twenty fourteen, Pfizer came calling with a takeover offer worth about a hundred seventeen billion dollars, at the time the largest attempted deal in pharmaceutical history. The board said no, and Soriot made a famous promise: AstraZeneca would reach forty-five billion dollars in sales by twenty twenty-three on its own. He was mocked for it. He beat it.

The rebuild ran through cancer medicine. Tagrisso, a targeted pill for lung cancers driven by mutations in the epidermal growth factor receptor, was approved in twenty fifteen and became the company's flagship, selling about seven and a quarter billion dollars in twenty twenty-five. Imfinzi, an immunotherapy antibody, established itself in stage three lung cancer and now sells around six billion. Lynparza, developed with Merck, pioneered a class of drugs targeting deoxyribonucleic acid repair weaknesses in ovarian, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancers. Calquence, from the twenty fifteen Acerta acquisition, became a multi-billion dollar leukemia franchise. And in twenty nineteen AstraZeneca paid Japan's Daiichi Sankyo up to nearly seven billion dollars to share Enhertu, an antibody-drug conjugate, essentially a guided missile that delivers chemotherapy directly to tumor cells, which is redefining treatment for breast cancer and sold nearly five billion dollars globally in twenty twenty-five.

Outside cancer, the diabetes pill Farxiga became something much bigger. Trials showed it protected failing hearts and kidneys regardless of diabetes, and by twenty twenty-five it was the company's single largest product at roughly eight and a half billion dollars. During the pandemic, AstraZeneca co-developed the Oxford coronavirus vaccine and supplied billions of doses at no profit, a genuine public-health achievement that nonetheless brought controversy over trial data, rare blood-clotting events, and a supply dispute with the European Union. And in twenty twenty-one the company closed its biggest acquisition ever: thirty-nine billion dollars for Alexion, the rare-disease specialist behind Soliris and its successor Ultomiris, drugs for devastating conditions like paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria and myasthenia gravis. Ultomiris now sells over four billion dollars a year.

So where is AstraZeneca placing its bets today? The stated ambition is eighty billion dollars in revenue by twenty thirty, and three big moves define the strategy. First, America. In July twenty twenty-five the company pledged fifty billion dollars of United States manufacturing and research investment, anchored by a four and a half billion dollar factory in Virginia. In October twenty twenty-five, Soriot stood in the Oval Office and struck a deal with the Trump administration: most-favored-nation pricing for Medicaid and discounted direct-to-consumer sales, in exchange for a three-year exemption from pharmaceutical tariffs. Shareholders also approved listing the company's shares directly on the New York Stock Exchange. Half of all revenue is meant to come from the United States by twenty thirty.

Second, a push into cardiometabolic disease and weight management. AstraZeneca is developing elecoglipron, an oral weight-loss pill in the same class as Ozempic and licensed from a Chinese biotech, plus an oral cholesterol pill targeting proprotein convertase subtilisin kexin type nine. Baxfendy, generic name baxdrostat, became the first approved aldosterone synthase inhibitor in May twenty twenty-six. It treats hard-to-control or resistant high blood pressure and came from the twenty twenty-three purchase of CinCor.

Third, deeper oncology. Camizestrant, a next-generation hormonal therapy already filed with regulators, could become the new backbone treatment for the most common form of breast cancer. The company sees it as a five billion dollar-plus opportunity. Datroway, a second antibody-drug conjugate with Daiichi Sankyo, was approved in twenty twenty-five for breast and lung cancer. AstraZeneca is also advancing two bispecific antibodies, volrustomig and rilvegostomig, as possible successors to Imfinzi, plus saruparib as a more selective successor to Lynparza. Outside oncology, Tezspire, partnered with Amgen, gained a possible new use when the Phase three Crossing trial met its main tissue-remission and swallowing-symptom endpoints in eosinophilic esophagitis. Numerical effect sizes and detailed safety data have not yet been disclosed. In rare disease, three medicines are with regulators: gefurulimab for generalized myasthenia gravis, efzimfotase alfa for hypophosphatasia, and eneboparatide for hypoparathyroidism.

Now the risks. The most immediate is Farxiga: American generic copies were approved in April twenty twenty-six, and China is adding the drug to its bulk-procurement price cuts, that is more than eight billion dollars of revenue now eroding. Lynparza follows later this decade. Further out, around twenty thirty-two, Tagrisso and Calquence face their own expirations. The growing dependence on America cuts both ways: the Inflation Reduction Act's Medicare price negotiations already touch Farxiga, and the pricing concessions to the White House compress margins. Then there is China, the company's second-largest market at over six billion dollars. AstraZeneca's former China president, Leon Wang, was detained in late twenty twenty-four, and in November twenty twenty-five Chinese prosecutors indicted him, another former employee, and the company's Chinese subsidiary on charges including illegal data collection and illegal drug importation. Remarkably, AstraZeneca has responded by investing more, a two and a half billion dollar research hub in Beijing and a pledge of over one hundred billion yuan by twenty thirty. Finally, the rare-disease business faces biosimilar copies of Soliris, and an ever-larger slice of revenue is shared with partners like Daiichi Sankyo and Merck, which weighs on margins.

The through-line of the AstraZeneca story is conviction under pressure: a company that refused a hundred-billion-dollar exit, rebuilt itself around cancer science, and is now betting it can outrun its next patent cliff just as it outran the last one. Whether the eighty billion dollar promise lands the way the forty-five billion one did is the question that will define its next five years.
