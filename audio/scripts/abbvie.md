AbbVie. If you want a single company that illustrates what a patent cliff looks like — and what surviving one looks like — this is it.

AbbVie is headquartered in North Chicago, Illinois. It was created in January twenty thirteen, when Abbott Laboratories split itself in two: Abbott kept the medical devices, diagnostics, and nutrition businesses, and AbbVie became an independent, research-based drug company. The name combines "Abbott" with "vie," from the Latin root for life. In twenty twenty-five, AbbVie booked about sixty-one billion dollars in revenue — a company record — with roughly fifty-seven thousand employees. The chief executive is Robert Michael, who took over from longtime leader Richard Gonzalez in the summer of twenty twenty-four.

AbbVie's story is dominated by one molecule: adalimumab, sold as Humira. Humira is an antibody that blocks a protein called tumor necrosis factor, a key driver of inflammation. Launched in two thousand three for rheumatoid arthritis, it steadily added approvals in psoriasis, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and more, and became the best-selling drug in the world. When AbbVie was spun off, Humira was roughly half its revenue, and everyone knew the patents would eventually run out. Essentially everything AbbVie has done since twenty thirteen has been about answering that problem.

The first answers were acquisitions, with mixed results. In twenty fourteen, AbbVie agreed to buy Shire for about fifty-four billion dollars, largely to lower its tax bill by moving its address abroad — then walked away when Washington changed the rules, paying a break-up fee of about one point six billion dollars. In twenty fifteen, it spent twenty-one billion dollars on Pharmacyclics to get a share of Imbruvica, or ibrutinib, a pill for chronic lymphocytic leukemia. That one worked commercially for years. In twenty sixteen, it spent nearly six billion dollars on a startup called Stemcentrx for a lung cancer drug that failed outright — a write-off that still gets cited as a cautionary tale. Internal research fared better: AbbVie co-developed venetoclax, sold as Venclexta, a first-of-its-kind pill for leukemia, and launched a very successful hepatitis C combination called Mavyret.

Meanwhile, AbbVie defended Humira with what critics called a patent thicket — well over a hundred patents layered on top of the original ones. Every biosimilar maker settled, agreeing to stay out of the United States until twenty twenty-three. Congress held hearings; an antitrust lawsuit was filed and ultimately dismissed on appeal. The strategy was controversial, but it bought AbbVie five extra years of American exclusivity, and Humira peaked at about twenty-one billion dollars in twenty twenty-two.

That bought time was used well, in two ways. First, in twenty nineteen and twenty twenty, AbbVie acquired Allergan for sixty-three billion dollars, bringing in Botox — both the medical version, used for chronic migraine and muscle disorders, and the famous cosmetic version — plus the antipsychotic Vraylar, the Juvederm filler line, and two migraine pills. Overnight, Humira went from half of revenue to about a third. Second, and more importantly, AbbVie launched two internally driven successors in twenty nineteen: Skyrizi, or risankizumab, an antibody against interleukin twenty-three for psoriasis and inflammatory bowel disease; and Rinvoq, or upadacitinib, a once-daily pill that inhibits an enzyme called Janus kinase one, approved across rheumatoid arthritis, eczema, and bowel disease.

Then came the cliff. American Humira biosimilars arrived in January twenty twenty-three, and Humira revenue collapsed from twenty-one billion dollars to about four and a half billion by twenty twenty-five. But the succession plan worked better than almost anyone predicted. In twenty twenty-five, Skyrizi alone brought in about seventeen and a half billion dollars, growing fifty percent, and Rinvoq added more than eight billion, growing nearly forty percent. Together, at about twenty-six billion dollars, they now out-earn Humira at its peak — and they carried AbbVie to record total revenue just two years after the cliff. It is the textbook example of managing a loss of exclusivity.

Not everything has gone smoothly. In late twenty twenty-three, AbbVie announced two big acquisitions: ImmunoGen, for about ten billion dollars, bringing the ovarian cancer antibody-drug conjugate Elahere; and Cerevel Therapeutics, for nearly nine billion, for neuroscience. Cerevel's crown jewel, a schizophrenia drug called emraclidine, failed both of its mid-stage trials in November twenty twenty-four, erasing roughly forty billion dollars of AbbVie's market value in a single morning and forcing a three and a half billion dollar write-down. The consolation prize was tavapadon, a once-daily Parkinson's pill from the same acquisition that has since passed three late-stage trials, with an approval decision expected in twenty twenty-six. AbbVie also won approval in late twenty twenty-four for Vyalev, a continuous under-the-skin infusion for advanced Parkinson's disease that is expected to pass a billion dollars in sales in twenty twenty-six.

So where is AbbVie placing its bets now? Immunology remains the core: in June twenty twenty-six it agreed to pay nearly eleven billion dollars for Apogee Therapeutics, whose lead antibody targets interleukin thirteen for eczema with dosing as infrequent as a few times a year. It bought Capstan Therapeutics for about two billion dollars for a technology that reprograms immune cells inside the body — a potential way to reset the immune system in autoimmune disease. In neuroscience, beyond Parkinson's, it is even betting about a billion dollars on a psychedelic-derived compound for depression. In cancer, it is building around antibody-drug conjugates — guided missiles that deliver chemotherapy directly to tumors — including Emrelis for lung cancer, its first home-grown one, plus licensed multi-targeting antibodies from partners in China and India. And it has entered the obesity race, licensing a long-acting amylin analog from Denmark's Gubra — a drug designed to suppress appetite through a different hormone pathway than the famous GLP-1 medicines.

The risks are worth stating plainly. AbbVie has traded one concentration for another: Skyrizi and Rinvoq are now more than forty percent of revenue, and their own patent expirations loom in the twenty thirties. Several mid-sized products — the leukemia pills Venclexta and Imbruvica, and the antipsychotic Vraylar — face expirations or government price cuts before then; Imbruvica's Medicare price was cut through negotiation starting in twenty twenty-six. The aesthetics business, led by Botox Cosmetic, has been shrinking as consumers pull back. And in January twenty twenty-six, AbbVie signed a pricing agreement with the White House — lower Medicaid prices and direct-to-consumer sales in exchange for tariff protection — alongside a pledge to invest one hundred billion dollars in American research and manufacturing over ten years.

The bottom line: AbbVie went over the biggest patent cliff in pharmaceutical history and came out bigger on the other side, thanks to a decade of preparation and two exceptional successor drugs. The question for the next decade is whether its aggressive deal-making — more than thirty transactions in two years — can do the same thing again before Skyrizi and Rinvoq face their own cliffs.
