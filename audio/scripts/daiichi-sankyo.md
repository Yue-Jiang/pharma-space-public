Today's company is Daiichi Sankyo, the Japanese pharmaceutical company that turned a single chemistry platform — the antibody-drug conjugate — into one of the most valuable franchises in modern oncology.

Let's start with the basics. Daiichi Sankyo is headquartered in Tokyo. It was formed in two thousand five through the merger of two venerable Japanese drugmakers: Sankyo, founded in eighteen ninety-nine, and Daiichi Pharmaceutical, founded in nineteen fifteen. In its most recent fiscal year, which ended in March of twenty twenty-six, the company booked about two point one trillion yen in revenue — call it roughly fourteen billion dollars — up nearly thirteen percent year over year. It employs around twenty thousand people, and its chief executive is Hiroyuki Okuzawa. The company is Japan's second-largest pharma, and today it is overwhelmingly an oncology story, though it still has a substantial cardiovascular business.

Both parent companies left real scientific legacies. Sankyo was where Akira Endo discovered the very first statin in the nineteen seventies, and its pravastatin became one of Japan's biggest drugs. Daiichi Pharmaceutical discovered the antibiotic levofloxacin. When the two merged, the combined company was anchored by a blood pressure pill called Benicar — generic name olmesartan — which at its peak provided about a quarter of total revenue.

That dependence set up the first big chapter of our story: the Ranbaxy disaster. In two thousand eight, anticipating the Benicar patent cliff, Daiichi Sankyo paid four point six billion dollars for a controlling stake in India's largest generics maker, Ranbaxy Laboratories. It was the biggest acquisition in Indian pharmaceutical history, and it went wrong almost immediately. Within months, the United States Food and Drug Administration barred imports from key Ranbaxy plants over falsified data and manufacturing failures. In twenty thirteen, Ranbaxy pleaded guilty in the United States and paid a record five hundred million dollars in fines. The next year Daiichi Sankyo gave up, selling Ranbaxy to Sun Pharmaceutical, and by twenty fifteen it had cashed out of India entirely — recovering perhaps three and a half billion dollars, well short of what it had paid. There was a measure of vindication in twenty sixteen, when a Singapore arbitration panel ordered Ranbaxy's former family owners to pay Daiichi Sankyo five hundred twenty-five million dollars for concealing the regulatory problems during the sale. The whole episode is now a classic business school case in acquisition diligence.

Meanwhile, the Benicar cliff arrived on schedule. United States exclusivity ended in twenty sixteen, and the franchise brought legal pain too: a thirty-nine million dollar kickback settlement with the American government in twenty fifteen, and a roughly three hundred million dollar product liability settlement in twenty seventeen over a rare intestinal side effect. The company cut jobs in Japan and the United States and leaned on its new blood thinner edoxaban — sold as Lixiana in most of the world and Savaysa in the United States — which grew into a franchise worth around three hundred fifty billion yen a year, mostly in Japan, Europe, and Asia.

But the real transformation came from the company's own laboratories. Through the twenty tens, Daiichi Sankyo quietly built an oncology pipeline through small acquisitions — a German biotech called U3 Pharma, the American companies Plexxikon and Ambit Biosciences — and, crucially, developed an in-house antibody-drug conjugate technology called D-X-d. An antibody-drug conjugate is essentially a guided missile: an antibody that homes in on a protein on cancer cells, carrying a potent chemotherapy payload that is released only when the antibody reaches its target. Daiichi Sankyo's version used a novel topoisomerase inhibitor payload attached by a cleverly designed cleavable linker, and it could carry roughly twice as many payload molecules per antibody as older conjugates.

The lead program paired this payload with trastuzumab, the antibody behind Herceptin, targeting a protein called HER2. When early trial data stunned the field in twenty seventeen, AstraZeneca came calling. In March twenty nineteen, AstraZeneca paid one point three five billion dollars upfront — in a deal worth up to six point nine billion — to co-develop and co-commercialize the drug, which became Enhertu. The two companies split costs and profits globally, except in Japan, where Daiichi Sankyo keeps exclusive rights and pays AstraZeneca a royalty. Enhertu won its first American approval in December twenty nineteen for advanced HER2-positive breast cancer, and then kept winning. It beat the previous standard of care head to head. It created an entirely new treatment category — HER2-low breast cancer — for patients whose tumors express only small amounts of the target. It added approvals in stomach cancer, lung cancer, and eventually any solid tumor expressing HER2. Combined sales with AstraZeneca reached three point seven five billion dollars in twenty twenty-four and continued climbing toward six billion in twenty twenty-five. In December twenty twenty-five it moved into first-line treatment of metastatic HER2-positive breast cancer, and in May twenty twenty-six it was approved for early-stage breast cancer, before and after surgery.

The partnership playbook worked so well that Daiichi Sankyo ran it twice more. In July twenty twenty, AstraZeneca paid up to six billion dollars for a second conjugate, datopotamab deruxtecan, now sold as Datroway, which targets a protein called TROP2 and is approved in breast and lung cancers. And in October twenty twenty-three, Merck paid four billion dollars upfront — up to twenty-two billion with milestones — for three more conjugates targeting proteins called HER3, B7-H3, and CDH6. Add it up, and Daiichi Sankyo has collected more upfront cash for its antibody-drug conjugates than most biotechs are worth, while keeping manufacturing, Japanese rights, and roughly half the economics.

It has not all been smooth. The HER3 conjugate, patritumab deruxtecan, was rejected once on manufacturing grounds, and in May twenty twenty-five its American application was withdrawn entirely after a confirmatory lung cancer trial failed to extend overall survival. Datroway's initial lung cancer strategy was scaled back. And for years the company fought a patent war with Seagen, the antibody-drug conjugate pioneer later bought by Pfizer, which claimed Enhertu infringed its linker patents. A Texas jury initially awarded Seagen about forty-two million dollars plus royalties, but in December twenty twenty-five a federal appeals court threw out the verdict and invalidated the patent — a complete victory for Daiichi Sankyo.

So where is the company going? The five-year plan announced in May twenty twenty-six targets more than three trillion yen in revenue by fiscal twenty thirty, and a place among the world's top five oncology companies by twenty thirty-five, with twenty new indication launches planned across five cancer medicines. The near-term pipeline includes the B7-H3 conjugate ifinatamab deruxtecan, under priority review in small cell lung cancer, and the CDH6 conjugate raludotatug deruxtecan in ovarian cancer.

The risks are worth remembering. Edoxaban, still worth around three hundred fifty billion yen a year, loses American patent protection around twenty twenty-eight and European protection around twenty thirty. Enhertu is roughly a third of revenue, and everything rides on one technology platform — a class-wide lung toxicity issue or a competitive leap by rivals, including cheaper conjugates licensed from China, would hurt everything at once. Half the profits flow to partners. And Enhertu itself loses American exclusivity around twenty thirty-three, so the next franchise has to arrive on time.

Still, step back and the arc is remarkable: a mid-sized Japanese cardiovascular company that stumbled through a disastrous Indian acquisition, absorbed a brutal patent cliff, and then — on the strength of its own chemistry — became the partner of choice for two of the world's biggest oncology companies and the standard-setter in one of medicine's hottest drug classes. That's Daiichi Sankyo.
