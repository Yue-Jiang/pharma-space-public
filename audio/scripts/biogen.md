Biogen. Let's talk about one of the original biotechnology companies — a firm that helped invent the industry, built an empire on multiple sclerosis, suffered one of the most spectacular failures in modern drug development, and is now reinventing itself through rare diseases, Alzheimer's, and immunology.

Biogen was founded in nineteen seventy-eight in Geneva, Switzerland, by a group of academic scientists that included two future Nobel Prize winners, Walter Gilbert and Phillip Sharp. Today it is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts, trades on the Nasdaq under the ticker B-I-I-B, employs roughly seven and a half thousand people, and in twenty twenty-five generated about nine point nine billion dollars in revenue — up about two percent, its first annual growth in several years. The chief executive is Christopher Viehbacher, the former head of Sanofi, who took over in late twenty twenty-two.

The company's first defining product arrived in nineteen ninety-six: Avonex, an interferon beta injection for relapsing multiple sclerosis. Avonex became the best-selling multiple sclerosis therapy of its era and turned Biogen into the world's leading multiple sclerosis company — an identity it kept for two decades.

In two thousand three, Biogen merged with IDEC Pharmaceuticals of San Diego in a stock deal worth roughly six point eight billion dollars. IDEC brought Rituxan, the pioneering cancer antibody developed with Genentech, and to this day Biogen collects a rich stream of royalties on that antibody family, including the multiple sclerosis blockbuster Ocrevus — worth close to two billion dollars a year.

Then came the Tysabri crisis. Tysabri, generic name natalizumab, is a potent antibody for multiple sclerosis approved in two thousand four. Just three months later, Biogen voluntarily pulled it from the market after patients developed a rare, often fatal brain infection called progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy. After building a strict safety monitoring program, the company brought Tysabri back in two thousand six, and it grew into a multi-billion-dollar product. The episode remains a textbook case of drug safety management.

The next boom was Tecfidera, generic name dimethyl fumarate, an oral pill for multiple sclerosis launched in twenty thirteen. It became one of the fastest drug launches in biotech history, peaking above four billion dollars a year. In twenty sixteen, Biogen added Spinraza, generic name nusinersen — the first-ever treatment for spinal muscular atrophy, a devastating genetic disease in infants and children. Licensed from Ionis Pharmaceuticals, Spinraza is an antisense oligonucleotide — a short strand of genetic material injected into the spinal fluid — and it validated an entire new class of medicine while becoming another franchise worth over one and a half billion dollars a year.

But two storms were gathering. First, the patent cliff: in June twenty twenty, a United States court invalidated Tecfidera's key patent, and generic copies flooded the market that August. United States Tecfidera sales collapsed. European protection lapsed in early twenty twenty-five, so the erosion continues today — Tecfidera fell nearly thirty percent in twenty twenty-five to about six hundred eighty million dollars. Biogen shifted patients to a follow-on pill called Vumerity, but the multiple sclerosis business — still about four billion dollars in twenty twenty-five — is in structural decline, and Tysabri now faces its own biosimilar competition.

Second, and more famously: Aduhelm. Biogen had bet heavily on aducanumab, an antibody designed to clear amyloid plaques from the brains of Alzheimer's patients. In March twenty nineteen the trials were halted for futility — then, months later, Biogen revived the program, arguing that one of the two studies was actually positive at high doses. In June twenty twenty-one, the Food and Drug Administration granted accelerated approval against the near-unanimous advice of its own advisory committee. Three committee members resigned in protest. Priced at fifty-six thousand dollars a year, Aduhelm faced a firestorm over its unproven benefit. In April twenty twenty-two, Medicare took the extraordinary step of restricting coverage to clinical trials, effectively killing the launch. The saga triggered a congressional investigation, billions in write-offs, the departure of the previous chief executive, and ultimately the drug's discontinuation in early twenty twenty-four. It is remembered as one of the costliest commercial failures in the industry's history.

Redemption in Alzheimer's came through a partnership. Leqembi, generic name lecanemab, is an anti-amyloid antibody developed with the Japanese company Eisai, who leads the program; the two companies split profits fifty-fifty. In July twenty twenty-three, Leqembi became the first Alzheimer's therapy to win full, traditional approval with clear evidence of slowing disease progression. Uptake has been gradual — global sales were around five hundred million dollars in twenty twenty-five — constrained by diagnostic and infusion logistics, and by competition from Eli Lilly's rival drug. A more convenient injectable version launched in late twenty twenty-five, and blood-based diagnostic tests are expected to expand the market.

Meanwhile, Christopher Viehbacher has been rebuilding the company around rare diseases and immunology. A restructuring program called Fit for Growth cut about one billion dollars in costs and roughly fourteen hundred jobs by the end of twenty twenty-five. In twenty twenty-three, Biogen paid seven point three billion dollars for Reata Pharmaceuticals to get Skyclarys, generic name omaveloxolone — the first treatment for Friedreich's ataxia, a rare inherited movement disorder — now generating over five hundred million dollars a year. The same year brought Zurzuvae, generic name zuranolone, developed with Sage Therapeutics: the first pill for postpartum depression, which is growing quickly. There is also Qalsody, generic name tofersen, for a genetic form of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and in twenty twenty-six regulators approved a new high-dose version of Spinraza to defend that franchise against gene-therapy competition.

The boldest move came in twenty twenty-six: Biogen acquired Apellis Pharmaceuticals for about five point six billion dollars upfront. Apellis brings two marketed medicines based on the same molecule, pegcetacoplan, which blocks part of the immune system called complement. Syfovre treats geographic atrophy, an advanced form of age-related vision loss, and Empaveli treats rare kidney diseases and a rare blood disorder. Together they sold about six hundred ninety million dollars in twenty twenty-five and are growing in the mid-to-high teens. The deal pushes Biogen into eye disease and kidney disease for the first time. Smaller deals followed the same logic: up to one billion dollars for RayThera, an immunology startup, and a hundred sixty-five million dollars upfront to Stoke Therapeutics for international rights to zorevunersen, a potential first disease-modifying treatment for Dravet syndrome, a severe childhood epilepsy.

The late-stage pipeline now carries the story. Litifilimab in lupus. Felzartamab in kidney transplant rejection and kidney disease. Zorevunersen in Dravet syndrome. Salanersen, a once-yearly successor to Spinraza that won the Food and Drug Administration's breakthrough therapy designation in June twenty twenty-six and is now in three global phase three trials. And diranersen, a tau-targeting Alzheimer's medicine developed with Ionis: in May twenty twenty-six its mid-stage trial missed its formal primary endpoint, yet showed the first evidence from a randomized study that lowering tau protein can clear tau pathology from the brain and slow cognitive decline — enough for Biogen to push it toward approval-stage trials. Multiple registrational readouts are expected within about a year.

The risks are real. Multiple sclerosis revenue keeps shrinking, and twenty twenty-six guidance calls for total revenue to decline by a mid-single-digit percentage. Tysabri and Spinraza each provide more than ten percent of revenue and both face competition. Medicare drug-pricing reform looms over its biggest products. The Apellis deal added meaningful debt, and a court judgment in late twenty twenty-five went against Biogen over past Tysabri royalties owed to Genentech.

So here is Biogen in a sentence: the company that built multiple sclerosis therapy, stumbled catastrophically with Aduhelm, and is now betting that Leqembi, rare-disease medicines, immunology, and the Apellis complement franchise can outgrow the melting core — a tale, as its own chief executive puts it, of two companies: one declining, one growing.
