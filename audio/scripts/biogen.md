Biogen is one of the original biotechnology companies: a firm that helped build the industry, became synonymous with multiple sclerosis, suffered one of modern drug development's most visible failures, and is now trying to reinvent itself through Alzheimer's disease, rare neurology, immunology, kidney disease, and eye care.

The company was founded in Geneva in nineteen seventy-eight by academic scientists including future Nobel laureates Walter Gilbert and Phillip Sharp. Today it is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts, trades on the Nasdaq under the ticker B I I B, and employs roughly seventy-five hundred people. Christopher Viehbacher, the former Sanofi chief executive, has led Biogen since November twenty twenty-two. Full-year twenty twenty-five revenue was about nine point nine billion dollars, up roughly two percent and marking the first annual growth in several years.

Biogen's first defining medicine was Avonex, generic name interferon beta one A, approved in nineteen ninety-six for relapsing multiple sclerosis. It became the best-selling multiple sclerosis treatment of its era and established the company's identity as a neurology specialist.

In two thousand three, Biogen merged with I D E C Pharmaceuticals in a stock deal valued at about six point eight billion dollars. I D E C brought Rituxan, generic name rituximab, the pioneering cancer antibody developed with Genentech. That legacy still matters because Biogen receives royalty and profit-share income from antibodies against cluster of differentiation twenty, usually called C D twenty. The family includes Rituxan, Ocrevus, and Gazyva, and the income stream is worth roughly one point eight billion dollars a year.

Then came the Tysabri crisis. Tysabri, generic name natalizumab, is a potent antibody for multiple sclerosis. It was approved in late two thousand four, then voluntarily withdrawn only months later after patients developed progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy, a rare and often fatal brain infection. Biogen rebuilt the launch around a strict safety-monitoring program, returned Tysabri to the market in two thousand six, and eventually turned it into a multibillion-dollar product. It still generated about one point seven billion dollars in twenty twenty-five, although biosimilar competition is now eroding sales.

The next boom was Tecfidera, generic name dimethyl fumarate, an oral multiple sclerosis medicine launched in twenty thirteen. It became one of biotech's fastest launches and peaked above four billion dollars a year. Biogen also introduced Spinraza, generic name nusinersen, in twenty sixteen. Licensed from Ionis, Spinraza was the first treatment for spinal muscular atrophy. It is an antisense oligonucleotide, a short strand of genetic material delivered into the spinal fluid, and it validated an important new class of medicine while growing into a franchise worth about one and a half billion dollars annually.

But two storms were gathering. First came the patent cliff. In June twenty twenty, a United States court invalidated Tecfidera's key patent. Generic copies arrived that August, and American sales collapsed. European protection ended in early twenty twenty-five. Tecfidera revenue fell almost thirty percent in twenty twenty-five to about six hundred eighty million dollars. Follow-on pill Vumerity, generic name diroximel fumarate, now brings in roughly seven hundred thirty million dollars, but it cannot reverse the structural decline of Biogen's multiple sclerosis portfolio.

The second storm was Aduhelm, generic name aducanumab. Biogen had bet heavily on this antibody, designed to clear amyloid plaques from the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease. Its pivotal trials were halted for futility in March twenty nineteen. Months later, Biogen revived the program, arguing that high-dose data from one of the two studies showed a benefit. In June twenty twenty-one, the Food and Drug Administration granted accelerated approval against the near-unanimous advice of its advisory committee. Three committee members resigned. The drug launched at fifty-six thousand dollars a year, but Medicare restricted coverage to clinical trials in April twenty twenty-two. The commercial launch effectively ended. The episode brought congressional scrutiny, billions in write-offs, deep cost cuts, and leadership change. Aduhelm was formally discontinued in early twenty twenty-four.

Biogen's second attempt in Alzheimer's has been more credible. Leqembi, generic name lecanemab, was developed with Eisai and won full Food and Drug Administration approval in July twenty twenty-three after showing that it slowed disease progression. Eisai leads the program and the partners split the economics equally. Global in-market sales reached roughly five hundred ten million dollars in twenty twenty-five. Adoption is still constrained by diagnosis, infusion capacity, and competition from Eli Lilly's Kisunla. Biogen and Eisai are addressing convenience with Leqembi Iqlik, a subcutaneous autoinjector. Maintenance dosing was approved in twenty twenty-five, and initiation dosing was approved in July twenty twenty-six.

The rest of the current portfolio shows the diversification effort. Skyclarys, generic name omaveloxolone, is the first treatment for Friedreich's ataxia and generated roughly five hundred twenty million dollars in twenty twenty-five. Biogen obtained it through the seven point three billion dollar acquisition of Reata Pharmaceuticals in twenty twenty-three. Zurzuvae, generic name zuranolone, is a pill for postpartum depression developed with Sage Therapeutics and brought in about two hundred million dollars. Qalsody, generic name tofersen, treats a genetic form of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. The company also sells several inflammatory-disease biosimilars with Samsung Bioepis.

The boldest strategic move arrived in twenty twenty-six, when Biogen bought Apellis Pharmaceuticals for about five point six billion dollars upfront, plus a contingent payment tied to sales. The deal added two medicines built on pegcetacoplan, an inhibitor of the complement immune pathway. Syfovre treats geographic atrophy, an advanced form of vision loss, while Empaveli treats rare blood and kidney diseases. Together they produced about six hundred ninety million dollars in twenty twenty-five. This pushes Biogen into ophthalmology and nephrology, adds meaningful debt, and makes the promised cost synergies important.

The pipeline now determines whether the reinvention works. Litifilimab and dapirolizumab pegol are in phase three development for lupus. Felzartamab, acquired with H I Bio, is an antibody being tested in transplant rejection and kidney diseases. Zorevunersen, licensed from Stoke outside North America, is in phase three for Dravet syndrome. Salanersen is a once-yearly potential successor to Spinraza and entered three phase three studies after receiving breakthrough designation in June twenty twenty-six. Diranersen, partnered with Ionis, attacks tau rather than amyloid in Alzheimer's disease. Its phase two study missed the formal primary endpoint, but reductions in tau pathology and a possible cognitive signal persuaded Biogen to plan registrational development.

The risks are straightforward. Multiple sclerosis revenue, still about four billion dollars in twenty twenty-five, keeps shrinking. Tysabri and Spinraza each contribute more than ten percent of revenue and both face competition. Leqembi uptake remains operationally difficult, its economics are shared, and Eisai controls development. Several important late-stage trials read out between late twenty twenty-six and late twenty twenty-seven. The Apellis transaction increased leverage, while pricing pressure and litigation remain part of the background.

Biogen's story is therefore a race between two businesses. The old multiple sclerosis company is declining. The newer company, built around Alzheimer's disease, rare neurology, immunology, and complement biology, must grow quickly enough to replace it. After Aduhelm, credibility has to come from data and execution, one program at a time.
