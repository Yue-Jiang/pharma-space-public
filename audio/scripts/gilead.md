Today's deep-dive is Gilead Sciences, the company that turned antiviral chemistry into one of the most profitable franchises in the history of medicine — and that has spent the last decade trying, with mixed success, to become more than an HIV company.

First, the snapshot. Gilead is headquartered in Foster City, California, and was founded in 1987 by Michael Riordan, a twenty-nine-year-old physician. It trades on the Nasdaq under the ticker G-I-L-D. In twenty twenty-five, Gilead brought in about twenty-nine point four billion dollars in total revenue and employed roughly seventeen thousand people. The chief executive is Daniel O'Day, who came over from Roche in twenty nineteen and also chairs the board. The company's center of gravity is virology — human immunodeficiency virus above all, plus liver disease — with a growing second business in cancer medicines, and early moves into inflammation.

To understand Gilead, understand one number: of its roughly twenty-nine billion dollars in product sales, nearly twenty-one billion comes from human immunodeficiency virus medicines. And nearly half of everything the company sells is a single product: Biktarvy, a once-daily single-tablet regimen that did fourteen point three billion dollars in twenty twenty-five and is the best-selling H-I-V treatment in history.

Now the story. Gilead spent its first decade as a small antiviral shop. Two things in nineteen ninety-six shaped its future: John Martin became chief executive, and the company licensed its flu drug oseltamivir to Roche, which sold it as Tamiflu and paid Gilead royalties for decades. In nineteen ninety-nine, Gilead bought NeXstar Pharmaceuticals, gaining the antifungal AmBisome and a real commercial organization.

The empire began in two thousand one with Viread, known generically as tenofovir disoproxil fumarate, which became the backbone of modern H-I-V therapy. Gilead then perfected a simple, powerful playbook: combine its antivirals into one daily pill. It bought Triangle Pharmaceuticals for about four hundred sixty-four million dollars to get emtricitabine, launched the two-drug combination Truvada in two thousand four, and in two thousand six, with Bristol Myers Squibb, launched Atripla — the first complete H-I-V regimen in a single daily tablet. Truvada later became the first pill approved to prevent H-I-V infection, an approach called pre-exposure prophylaxis, or PrEP, which created an entirely new market.

Then came the deal that defines Gilead. In late twenty eleven, it paid eleven point two billion dollars for Pharmasset, a company with no products on the market, to get a hepatitis C compound called sofosbuvir. Wall Street thought Gilead had overpaid. Instead, Sovaldi launched in December twenty thirteen as an actual cure for hepatitis C — and at eighty-four thousand dollars per course of treatment, it ignited a national firestorm over drug pricing, including a Senate investigation. It was famously called the thousand-dollar pill. The follow-on combination Harvoni made things even bigger. Hepatitis C revenue peaked around nineteen billion dollars in twenty fifteen, pushing total company revenue past thirty-two billion. And then gravity took over — because when you cure patients, they stop needing your drug. The hepatitis C business shrank year after year as the pool of patients emptied and competitors slashed prices. Gilead has spent the decade since trying to replace that revenue.

While hepatitis C deflated, Gilead executed a quieter, brilliant maneuver in H-I-V: it switched its whole franchise from the older tenofovir to a gentler version called tenofovir alafenamide, launching Genvoya, Descovy, Odefsey, and Vemlidy in the mid twenty-tens — just before the old patents expired. Biktarvy arrived in twenty eighteen and conquered the market.

The diversification hunt was rockier. In twenty seventeen, Gilead paid eleven point nine billion dollars for Kite Pharma, entering cell therapy with Yescarta, a treatment that re-engineers a patient's own immune cells to attack lymphoma. In twenty twenty, it made its biggest bet ever: twenty-one billion dollars for Immunomedics and the antibody-drug conjugate Trodelvy, now a blockbuster in breast cancer. But there were expensive failures too: a five billion dollar collaboration with Galapagos whose lead arthritis drug was rejected in the United States; the four point nine billion dollar Forty Seven acquisition, whose lead cancer drug was abandoned; a failed push into fatty liver disease; and Trodelvy's bladder cancer approval was withdrawn in twenty twenty-four. The pandemic brought a windfall — Veklury, or remdesivir, the first approved COVID-nineteen antiviral, peaked at over five billion dollars a year — but that has faded to under a billion. There was litigation as well: Gilead paid forty million dollars in twenty twenty-four to settle claims that it delayed its safer tenofovir formulation to protect profits on the older one, and it won a jury verdict against the United States government in a patent fight over Truvada for prevention.

That brings us to the present, and to lenacapavir — arguably the most important new H-I-V medicine in a generation. Lenacapavir is an injection given just twice a year. In prevention trials it was essentially one hundred percent effective, a result the journal Science named its Breakthrough of the Year. Approved in June twenty twenty-five under the brand name Yeztugo, it costs about twenty-eight thousand dollars a year in the United States and booked one hundred fifty million dollars in its first half-year. Globally, Gilead struck a no-profit deal with the Global Fund to supply up to two million people, and licensed generic makers to sell it for about forty dollars a year in one hundred twenty lower-income countries starting in twenty twenty-seven.

Strategically, Gilead is in an unusually comfortable position: after settling with generic challengers, Biktarvy faces no United States generics until April twenty thirty-six, meaning no major patent cliff for a decade. Management is spending that breathing room aggressively. In early twenty twenty-six alone, Gilead announced nearly thirteen billion dollars of acquisitions: seven point eight billion for Arcellx, whose cell therapy anito-cel for multiple myeloma awaits a regulatory decision in December twenty twenty-six; two point two billion for Ouro Medicines and its T-cell engager technology; and over three billion for Tubulis, a German specialist in next-generation antibody-drug conjugates. Twenty twenty-six could see four launches: Trodelvy in first-line metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, anito-cel, a once-daily combination of bictegravir and lenacapavir for treatment, and the hepatitis delta drug bulevirtide in the United States. Beyond that sits a once-weekly oral regimen partnered with Merck, and even once-yearly dosing of lenacapavir.

So what are the risks? Concentration, first: nearly three quarters of sales come from H-I-V, and half from one product. Second, a nearer-term cliff around twenty thirty-one to twenty thirty-two for the tenofovir alafenamide family — Descovy, Genvoya, Odefsey, and Vemlidy — worth several billion dollars combined. Third, United States pricing policy: the Medicare Part D redesign cost Gilead about one point one billion dollars in twenty twenty-five, and in December twenty twenty-five the company signed a most-favored-nation pricing agreement with the administration, trading Medicaid discounts and pricing commitments for tariff relief. Cuts to public health funding for prevention could also slow Yeztugo. Fourth, the cancer business still has to prove itself: the cell therapies Yescarta and Tecartus are losing ground to newer competitors, and anito-cel launches into a crowded myeloma market.

The through-line of the Gilead story is a company that repeatedly bet the house on antiviral science and won — Viread, Sovaldi, Biktarvy, lenacapavir — while its attempts to buy its way into other fields have been slower and costlier. With a decade of patent security in hand and a prevention drug that may reshape the epidemic, the next chapter is about whether oncology finally becomes Gilead's second act.
