Takeda. Japan's largest drugmaker, and one of the oldest pharmaceutical companies on Earth.

The company traces its origins to seventeen eighty-one, when Chobei Takeda the first began selling traditional medicines in the Doshomachi district of Osaka, the old medicine-sellers' quarter. Nearly two and a half centuries later, Takeda is a global biopharmaceutical company with roughly fifty thousand employees, listed in Tokyo and New York, and fiscal year twenty twenty-five revenue of about four and a half trillion yen, roughly twenty-eight billion dollars. Its focus areas are gastroenterology and inflammation, rare diseases, plasma-derived therapies, oncology, vaccines, and neuroscience.

Let's start with what Takeda sells today. The flagship is Entyvio, generic name vedolizumab, an antibody for ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease. It brings in around six and a half billion dollars a year, about a fifth of company revenue, helped by a newer under-the-skin injection pen. Next comes the plasma business: immunoglobulin products like Gammagard, HyQvia, and Cuvitru, made from donated human plasma and used for immune deficiencies. Together they generate over five billion dollars. Then Takhzyro, generic name lanadelumab, an antibody that prevents attacks of hereditary angioedema, a rare swelling disorder, with roughly a billion and a half dollars a year. The rest of the portfolio includes the dengue fever vaccine Qdenga, the colorectal cancer pill Fruzaqla, the leukemia drug Iclusig, the lung cancer drug Alunbrig, the transplant antiviral Livtencity, and, in Japan and Asia, the acid-blocker Takecab.

Now the history, because Takeda's story is really the story of how a Japanese national champion forced itself to become a global company.

Through the nineteen nineties, Takeda prospered on four blockbusters: Prevacid for acid reflux, Actos for type two diabetes, a blood pressure drug called candesartan, and a prostate cancer treatment. Much of that success ran through an American joint venture with Abbott. But by the mid two thousands, every one of those drugs was heading off patent, and Takeda had little behind them.

So Takeda bought its way forward. In two thousand eight, it paid nearly nine billion dollars for Millennium Pharmaceuticals in Boston, gaining the cancer drug Velcade and, just as important, an American research hub that remains the heart of Takeda research and development today. In twenty eleven it spent close to fourteen billion dollars on Nycomed of Switzerland, buying reach into Europe and emerging markets. The old blockbusters went out painfully. Actos, once a multi-billion dollar diabetes franchise, was hit by lawsuits claiming it caused bladder cancer; a jury initially awarded nine billion dollars in punitive damages, later drastically reduced, and Takeda settled most cases for around two point four billion dollars in twenty fifteen.

The real transformation began in twenty fourteen, when Takeda hired Christophe Weber from GlaxoSmithKline, the first non-Japanese leader in the company's history. Weber globalized the executive team, focused research on a few core areas, and in twenty seventeen bought Ariad Pharmaceuticals for about five billion dollars, adding two targeted cancer medicines.

Then came the bet that defined the modern company. In twenty eighteen, Takeda agreed to buy Shire, the Irish-headquartered rare disease specialist, for sixty-two billion dollars. It was the largest foreign acquisition ever made by a Japanese company, closer to eighty billion including debt. Shire brought the rare disease portfolio, the plasma business, and the attention deficit hyperactivity disorder blockbuster Vyvanse. The deal doubled Takeda's revenue past thirty billion dollars, but it also loaded the balance sheet with enormous debt. Takeda spent the next five years selling about thirteen billion dollars of non-core assets, including its dry-eye drug to Novartis for over five billion and its Japanese consumer health unit to Blackstone, to pay that debt down.

And the whole time, a clock was ticking. Vyvanse, which had grown to more than three billion dollars a year, lost its United States patent protection in August of twenty twenty-three. Generics arrived within days, and most of that revenue evaporated over the next two years. Twenty twenty-three brought other setbacks too: a lung cancer drug was withdrawn from the market after a failed confirmatory study, a cell therapy for Crohn's disease failed late-stage testing, and Takeda pulled its United States application for the dengue vaccine. The company answered with a multi-year restructuring that has delivered roughly two billion dollars in annualized savings.

Which brings us to today, and the reason Takeda is interesting right now: the handoff. Fiscal year twenty twenty-five, which ended in March of twenty twenty-six, saw revenue dip slightly as the last of the Vyvanse erosion washed through. Management describes six late-stage assets as having a combined peak sales potential of ten to twenty billion dollars.

Three launches anchor the story. First, and biggest: Orzeyful, generic name oveporexton, approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration in August twenty twenty-six, with launch pending controlled-substance scheduling. It is the first drug that treats the underlying cause of narcolepsy type one, a missing brain signaling molecule called orexin, rather than just the symptoms. Takeda discovered it in-house and sees it as the start of an entire orexin franchise. Second: rusfertide, licensed from Protagonist Therapeutics for three hundred million dollars upfront, is a peptide for the blood disorder polycythemia vera, under priority review with a launch expected in late twenty twenty-six. Third: zasocitinib, an oral pill for psoriasis that Takeda acquired from Nimbus Therapeutics for four billion dollars upfront, has positive late-stage data and a launch planned for twenty twenty-seven.

Takeda has also become a notable channel for Chinese innovation: its colorectal cancer drug Fruzaqla was licensed from HutchMed of Shanghai, and it holds an option on a next-generation leukemia drug from Ascentage Pharma.

Leadership changed hands in June twenty twenty-six. Julie Kim, an American who joined through the Shire acquisition and ran Takeda's United States business, succeeded the retiring Christophe Weber, becoming the company's first woman chief executive.

So what are the risks? Three stand out. First, concentration: Entyvio is a fifth of revenue, and biosimilar copies are expected around twenty thirty-one. The new launches must scale before that cliff arrives. Second, litigation: in May twenty twenty-six a Boston jury found Takeda liable for illegally paying a generic maker to delay competition to its constipation drug Amitiza, awarding damages that treble to roughly two point seven billion dollars. Takeda booked a provision that nearly erased its reported operating profit for the year, and is appealing. Third, execution: three simultaneous launches into competitive markets is hard, and Takeda's recent record includes several late-stage failures alongside its wins.

The way to remember Takeda: a two-hundred-forty-year-old Osaka medicine seller that made one enormous, debt-fueled bet on Shire to become global, spent five years digesting it, absorbed the loss of Vyvanse, and is now betting that orexin science, a psoriasis pill, and a rare blood disease drug can carry it through the twenty thirties, under its first American chief executive.

That's Takeda.
