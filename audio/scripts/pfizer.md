# Pfizer — audio deep-dive

Pfizer is the archetypal big pharma company: the oldest of the American giants, founded in Brooklyn in eighteen forty-nine, headquartered today in Manhattan, and trading on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker P F E. In fiscal year twenty twenty-five it booked about sixty-two point six billion dollars in revenue with roughly seventy-five thousand employees. Albert Bourla, a Greek veterinarian by training who spent his whole career at the company, has been chief executive since twenty nineteen and also chairs the board. Pfizer's focus areas today are cancer medicines, vaccines, internal medicine — which now means the race for obesity drugs — inflammation and immunology, and rare diseases.

Let's start with what Pfizer actually sells. Its biggest product is Eliquis, generic name apixaban, a blood thinner that prevents strokes in people with atrial fibrillation. Pfizer shares it with Bristol Myers Squibb, and Pfizer's share came to about eight billion dollars in twenty twenty-five. Second is the Prevnar family of pneumococcal vaccines, which protect against the bacteria that cause pneumonia and meningitis — about six and a half billion dollars. Close behind is the Vyndaqel family, generic name tafamidis, sold in the United States mainly as Vyndamax. It treats transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy, a once-obscure disease in which misfolded proteins stiffen the heart muscle; diagnosis rates have soared, and the franchise brought in about six point four billion dollars, growing double digits. Fourth is Comirnaty, the COVID-nineteen vaccine developed with BioNTech, at about four point four billion dollars — a fraction of its pandemic peak. And fifth is Ibrance, generic name palbociclib, a pill for the most common form of metastatic breast cancer, at about four point one billion dollars and declining as its patent expiry approaches. Behind those come the COVID antiviral Paxlovid, the prostate cancer drug Xtandi shared with Astellas, the bladder cancer antibody-drug conjugate Padcev, the migraine pill Nurtec, and the respiratory syncytial virus vaccine Abrysvo.

Now the history, because Pfizer's story explains almost every big-pharma strategy debate of the last thirty years. In the late nineteen nineties, Pfizer rode primary-care blockbusters — most famously Viagra — but its defining move was hostile. Pfizer co-promoted a cholesterol pill called Lipitor, generic name atorvastatin, owned by Warner-Lambert. When Warner-Lambert tried to merge with another company in nineteen ninety-nine, Pfizer counter-attacked and swallowed Warner-Lambert whole for about ninety billion dollars. Lipitor became the best-selling drug in pharmaceutical history, peaking near thirteen billion dollars a year. Pfizer repeated the playbook in two thousand three, buying Pharmacia for about sixty billion dollars.

But scale could not manufacture science. The painkiller Bextra was withdrawn for safety in two thousand five. Torcetrapib, the heart drug meant to succeed Lipitor, was abandoned in two thousand six when a late-stage trial showed it increased deaths. And in two thousand nine, Pfizer paid two point three billion dollars to settle charges of illegal off-label marketing — at the time, the largest healthcare fraud settlement in American history. That same year, staring down the loss of the Lipitor patent, Pfizer bought Wyeth for sixty-eight billion dollars, which brought the Prevnar vaccine business and biologics know-how. When Lipitor's patent expired in twenty eleven, revenue slid for years, and Pfizer slimmed down — selling its nutrition business to Nestlé, spinning off its animal health unit as Zoetis, and later splitting off its off-patent generics business, Upjohn, which merged with Mylan to become Viatris. Two attempts to move its tax domicile abroad — a rebuffed bid for AstraZeneca and a one-hundred-sixty-billion-dollar merger with Allergan — collapsed, the latter killed by new Treasury rules in twenty sixteen.

Then came the pandemic, and with it the greatest windfall in industry history. Pfizer partnered with the German biotech BioNTech and delivered the first authorized COVID-nineteen vaccine in December twenty twenty, following it with the antiviral pill Paxlovid. In twenty twenty-two Pfizer's revenue hit one hundred point three billion dollars — the highest annual revenue any pharmaceutical company has ever recorded. Just as quickly, it unwound. COVID demand collapsed, twenty twenty-three revenue fell to fifty-eight and a half billion, and the share price sank below pre-pandemic levels. Pfizer had spent the windfall on acquisitions: the migraine company Biohaven, the sickle-cell company Global Blood Therapeutics, and above all Seagen, the leader in antibody-drug conjugates — targeted chemotherapy delivered by antibodies — for forty-three billion dollars in December twenty twenty-three. Some bets soured fast: the sickle-cell drug Oxbryta was withdrawn worldwide in twenty twenty-four, an activist investor took a billion-dollar stake to protest capital allocation, and in April twenty twenty-five Pfizer scrapped danuglipron, its home-grown obesity pill, after a liver injury case.

That brings us to the current strategy, which rests on three big bets. The first is obesity. Having lost its internal candidate, Pfizer went shopping and won a rare public bidding war against Novo Nordisk for Metsera, a startup with next-generation weight-loss injections. The deal closed in November twenty twenty-five at about seven billion dollars in cash, or up to roughly ten billion with milestone payments. Metsera brings a monthly injectable glucagon-like peptide-one drug entering Phase three, and a monthly amylin analog — a different hormone pathway — in early trials. Management admits the deal dilutes earnings through twenty thirty; it is a long-dated wager on pharma's biggest growth market.

The second bet is oncology, where Pfizer aims for eight or more blockbuster cancer medicines by twenty thirty. The pipeline includes sasanlimab, an immunotherapy with positive Phase three data in bladder cancer; atirmociclib, the designed successor to Ibrance in breast cancer; new antibody-drug conjugates from the Seagen engine; and notably S S G J seven-oh-seven, an antibody targeting two cancer pathways at once, licensed from the Chinese company Three S Bio in May twenty twenty-five for one and a quarter billion dollars upfront plus up to four point eight billion in milestones — one of the largest China-originated licensing deals ever signed.

The third bet is political. On September thirtieth, twenty twenty-five, Pfizer became the first drugmaker to sign a most-favored-nation pricing agreement with the Trump administration — matching the lowest prices charged in other wealthy countries for Medicaid, selling discounted drugs directly to consumers through the TrumpRx website, and pledging seventy billion dollars of American investment, all in exchange for a three-year exemption from threatened one-hundred-percent pharmaceutical tariffs.

Now the risks, and they are substantial. Pfizer faces the industry's largest patent cliff: between now and roughly twenty thirty, Eliquis, Vyndaqel, Ibrance, Xtandi, and parts of the Prevnar franchise — together over twenty billion dollars of current annual revenue — lose exclusivity. The COVID franchise keeps shrinking, and American vaccine policy has turned less favorable. Government pricing pressure is compounding: Eliquis got a negotiated Medicare price starting January twenty twenty-six, with Ibrance and Xtandi next in line. And the pipeline carries concentration risk — the obesity bet sits in unproven clinical-stage assets, and there have been recent trial misses in lung cancer.

The way to remember Pfizer: it is the industry's great acquirer, a company that has repeatedly bought its next act — Warner-Lambert for Lipitor, Wyeth for vaccines, BioNTech's partnership for COVID, Seagen for cancer, Metsera for obesity. Twice in its history a windfall product masked a thin pipeline, and twice the cliff that followed forced reinvention. Whether the current rebuild — cancer, obesity, and a seat at the political table — fills the coming gap is the central question hanging over the world's most famous drug company.
