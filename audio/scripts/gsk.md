This is the deep-dive on GSK, the British pharmaceutical company headquartered in London.

Let's start with the numbers. In twenty twenty-five, GSK reported revenue of about thirty-two point seven billion pounds — roughly forty-three billion dollars — up seven percent at constant exchange rates. The company employs about sixty-seven thousand people and trades in London and New York under the ticker G-S-K. At the start of twenty twenty-six, Luke Miels took over as chief executive from Dame Emma Walmsley, who had run the company for nine years. GSK today is organized around three buckets: specialty medicines, which is the growth engine at thirteen and a half billion pounds; vaccines, at just over nine billion pounds; and general medicines, mostly older products, at ten billion pounds.

Where did this company come from? GSK is the product of a long chain of mergers. Glaxo began as a dried-milk business in New Zealand and became a pharmaceutical powerhouse thanks to Zantac, the stomach-acid drug that in the late nineteen-eighties was the best-selling medicine in the world. In nineteen ninety-five, Glaxo bought Wellcome for around nine billion pounds, picking up a pioneering H-I-V business. On the other side, SmithKline Beecham combined an American and a British firm, bringing the antibiotic Augmentin, the antidepressant Paxil, and a vaccines business. In two thousand, Glaxo Wellcome and SmithKline Beecham merged in a deal valued at roughly seventy-five billion dollars, creating GlaxoSmithKline — briefly the largest drug company on earth.

The two-thousands were dominated by one inhaler: Advair, a combination treatment for asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease that peaked around eight billion dollars a year. But the decade also brought serious trouble. The diabetes drug Avandia was linked to heart risks in two thousand seven and effectively collapsed. In twenty twelve, GSK paid three billion dollars in what was then the largest healthcare-fraud settlement in American history, over improper marketing and withheld safety data. Two years later, a Chinese court fined the company nearly half a billion dollars for bribery. One bright spot from this era proved enormously important: in two thousand nine GSK pooled its H-I-V assets with Pfizer to create ViiV Healthcare, whose drug dolutegravir became the backbone of modern H-I-V therapy worldwide.

Under chief executive Andrew Witty, GSK diversified. The signature move came in twenty fourteen and fifteen: a three-part asset swap with Novartis, in which GSK sold its cancer drugs for sixteen billion dollars, bought Novartis's vaccines business, and merged consumer health into a joint venture. That made GSK a giant in vaccines and consumer products — but left it without a cancer business, a decision it would later spend billions reversing.

Emma Walmsley took over in twenty seventeen and pointed the company back at innovative medicines. Her era brought the shingles vaccine Shingrix, which became a multi-billion-pound blockbuster; the five-billion-dollar acquisition of Tesaro to re-enter oncology; and, most consequentially, the split of the company in July twenty twenty-two, when the consumer business was spun off as Haleon, leaving GSK a pure biopharmaceutical company. She also closed the book on Zantac litigation in October twenty twenty-four, agreeing to pay up to two point two billion dollars to settle about eighty thousand American state-court cases — without admitting liability. And she went shopping: Affinivax for pneumococcal vaccines, Sierra Oncology for the bone-marrow drug momelotinib, Bellus Health for a chronic-cough drug, and several licensing deals with Chinese biotechs.

So what does GSK actually sell today? The biggest product is Shingrix, the shingles vaccine, at three point six billion pounds in twenty twenty-five. Next is Trelegy, a once-daily triple-therapy inhaler for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma, at three billion pounds. The H-I-V franchise brought in seven point seven billion pounds, led by Dovato, a two-drug pill combining dolutegravir and lamivudine, at about two point seven billion pounds, and increasingly by long-acting injectables: Cabenuva for treatment and Apretude for prevention, both growing at forty to sixty percent a year. In respiratory and immunology, Nucala — an antibody against interleukin-five for severe asthma and now chronic obstructive pulmonary disease — passed two billion pounds, and Benlysta, for lupus, reached almost one point eight billion. The rebuilt cancer business is small but the fastest-growing part of the company, up forty-three percent: Jemperli, an immunotherapy for endometrial cancer, nearly doubled to over eight hundred million pounds, and Ojjaara, for the bone-marrow cancer myelofibrosis, grew sixty percent. And there's a comeback story: Blenrep, an antibody-drug conjugate for multiple myeloma that was withdrawn from the American market in twenty twenty-two after a failed confirmatory trial, was re-approved by the American regulator in October twenty twenty-five, though with a narrower label than hoped.

Vaccines beyond Shingrix tell a more cautionary tale. Arexvy, the world's first vaccine against respiratory syncytial virus, launched spectacularly in twenty twenty-three at about one point two billion pounds — then fell by half after American advisory committees narrowed their recommendations. It's a reminder that United States vaccine policy is now a genuine business risk for GSK's nine-billion-pound vaccine segment.

What are the big bets going forward? First, ultra-long-acting biologics. In December twenty twenty-five the company won American approval for Exdensur, known generically as depemokimab — the first asthma biologic dosed just twice a year. GSK thinks it can reach four billion dollars in peak sales. Second, hepatitis B. Bepirovirsen, an antisense medicine licensed from Ionis, showed in phase three trials that a six-month course can achieve functional cure in roughly one in five patients — something no existing therapy does — with first regulatory decisions expected in late twenty twenty-six. Third, China-originated drugs. In July twenty twenty-five GSK signed one of the industry's biggest China licensing deals: five hundred million dollars upfront to Hengrui Pharma for up to twelve programs, potentially worth twelve billion dollars, headlined by a new inhaled-class drug for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Fourth, rebuilding oncology with antibody-drug conjugates and earlier lines of therapy for Blenrep and Jemperli.

Not everything works. In July twenty twenty-six, GSK abandoned camlipixant, the chronic-cough drug it had acquired with Bellus Health for two billion dollars, after mixed phase three results. It had earlier written off almost half a billion pounds on a cancer immunotherapy program. Pipeline attrition is the tax on this strategy.

The key risks to hold in mind. Number one, the dolutegravir patent cliff: core patents expire in twenty twenty-seven and twenty twenty-eight, and dolutegravir-based products brought in about five point seven billion dollars in twenty twenty-five — nearly a quarter of company revenue sits in H-I-V. The bridge is long-acting injectables, but the gap is real. Number two, vaccine softness: Shingrix has plateaued in the United States and Arexvy has already shown how quickly policy changes can cut a franchise in half. Number three, Trelegy faces potential American generic competition around twenty thirty. Number four, American drug-pricing reform is already squeezing prices through the Medicare redesign. And number five, execution: management has promised more than forty billion pounds of revenue by twenty thirty-one — several billion above what analysts currently model — so the pipeline has to convert.

The bottom line: GSK is a focused biopharmaceutical company with world-class vaccines and H-I-V franchises, a genuinely improving pipeline in respiratory disease, hepatitis B, and oncology — and a hard deadline. How well the new chief executive, Luke Miels, manages the dolutegravir cliff while landing the next wave of launches will define whether GSK's post-Haleon transformation is judged a success.
