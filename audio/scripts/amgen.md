# Amgen — audio deep-dive

Amgen is the original biotechnology giant — the company that proved genetically engineered proteins could be a business. Founded in nineteen eighty as Applied Molecular Genetics, it is headquartered in Thousand Oaks, California, and trades on the Nasdaq under the ticker A M G N. In fiscal year twenty twenty-five it booked about thirty-six point eight billion dollars in total revenue, up ten percent, with roughly thirty-one and a half thousand employees. Robert Bradway, an investment banker turned operator, has been chairman and chief executive since twenty twelve — one of the longest tenures in large-cap pharma. The focus areas are general medicine, meaning cardiovascular disease and bone health; cancer and blood disorders; inflammation; rare disease; a fast-growing biosimilars unit selling copies of rivals' biologics; and, as the emerging bet, obesity.

Amgen's portfolio is unusually broad — fourteen products cleared a billion dollars in twenty twenty-five. The biggest is Prolia, generic name denosumab, an antibody for osteoporosis, at about four point four billion dollars. Its twin, Xgeva — the same molecule at a different dose, used to protect bones in cancer patients — adds another two point one billion. Together they are also the company's biggest problem: the United States patent expired in February twenty twenty-five, biosimilar copies have launched, and erosion is expected to accelerate. The second pillar is Repatha, generic name evolocumab, an antibody that dramatically lowers L D L cholesterol — the so-called bad cholesterol — by blocking a protein called P C S K nine. Repatha grew thirty-six percent to three billion dollars, and a late twenty twenty-five outcomes trial showed it cuts heart attacks and strokes even in people who have not yet had one, opening a much larger market. Third is Otezla, generic name apremilast, a psoriasis pill acquired from Celgene, at about two point three billion. Fourth is Enbrel, generic name etanercept, the pioneering anti-inflammatory biologic that came with the Immunex acquisition — once a five-billion-dollar franchise, now down a third to two point two billion after Medicare price negotiation cut its price by roughly two-thirds. Fifth is Evenity, generic name romosozumab, a bone-building osteoporosis antibody growing thirty-four percent to two point one billion. And sixth is Tepezza, generic name teprotumumab, the first medicine for thyroid eye disease, at about one point nine billion. Behind those sit the leukemia bispecific Blincyto, up twenty-eight percent; the severe asthma antibody Tezspire, partnered with AstraZeneca and up fifty-two percent; the gout infusion Krystexxa; and the rare-disease antibody Uplizna.

Now the history. Amgen's foundation was two recombinant proteins: Epogen, launched in nineteen eighty-nine, which stimulates red blood cell production in dialysis patients, and Neupogen, launched in nineteen ninety-one, which boosts infection-fighting white blood cells in chemotherapy patients. Through the nineteen nineties these anemia and immune-support franchises made Amgen biotechnology's biggest success story, and in two thousand one it extended both with longer-acting successors, Aranesp and Neulasta. In two thousand two Amgen bought Immunex for about sixteen billion dollars — then the largest biotechnology deal ever — gaining Enbrel. By two thousand six revenue had roughly tripled to over fourteen billion dollars, with the anemia franchise alone approaching seven billion.

Then came the existential shock. In two thousand seven, studies linked high doses of the anemia drugs to cardiovascular events and tumor progression. Regulators imposed their sternest warnings, Medicare slashed reimbursement, and Aranesp sales collapsed. Amgen cut about fourteen percent of its staff, and in twenty twelve pleaded guilty to illegally marketing Aranesp, paying seven hundred sixty-two million dollars — then the largest such settlement by a biotechnology company. The rebuild came through denosumab: Prolia and Xgeva were both approved in twenty ten and grew into the multi-billion-dollar franchise now facing its own expiry.

Under Bradway, from twenty twelve, the playbook became disciplined bolt-on deals. Micromet brought the bispecific antibody platform behind Blincyto. DeCODE Genetics, an Icelandic human-genetics company, gave Amgen a genetic evidence engine for choosing drug targets. Onyx Pharmaceuticals, at ten point four billion dollars, brought the multiple myeloma drug Kyprolis. Repatha was approved in twenty fifteen but initially stalled against insurer resistance; a big outcomes trial and aggressive list-price cuts eventually unlocked today's growth. Amgen also launched a biosimilars business in twenty sixteen — copying drugs like Humira, Stelara, Eylea, and Soliris — even as its own legacy products absorbed biosimilar erosion. In twenty nineteen it bought Otezla for thirteen point four billion dollars and took a twenty percent stake in China's BeiGene, now BeOne Medicines. In twenty twenty-one came Lumakras, the first ever approved drug against a notorious cancer mutation called K R A S G twelve C — a landmark of chemistry, though its commercial run has been bumpier. The defining move was Horizon Therapeutics, closed in October twenty twenty-three for twenty-seven point eight billion dollars — Amgen's largest acquisition — which added Tepezza, Krystexxa, and Uplizna and built a rare-disease business now above four and a half billion dollars a year.

That brings us to the current strategy, and one bet towers over the rest: obesity. MariTide, generic name maridebart cafraglutide, is a monthly — or possibly even less frequent — injectable that combines a glucagon-like peptide-one agonist with an antibody blocking a second gut-hormone receptor. Phase two data showed roughly twelve to sixteen percent average weight loss at one year without plateauing, and a large Phase three program called MARITIME is underway in obesity, diabetes, heart disease, heart failure, and sleep apnea. The pitch is convenience: a monthly shot against the weekly injections from Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk. It is a genuine challenger position — but Amgen is years behind two entrenched giants. Alongside obesity, the company is expanding Repatha into prevention for people who haven't yet had heart attacks, running a Phase three program for olpasiran, a gene-silencing drug against lipoprotein little-a, an inherited cholesterol-like risk factor; pushing its bispecific cancer engagers, including the small cell lung cancer drug Imdelltra and a prostate candidate called xaluritamig; and growing the rare-disease and biosimilar businesses. Notably, Amgen has sat out the industry's wave of China licensing deals — its Asia position is the BeOne equity stake.

Now the risks. The denosumab cliff is the biggest: Prolia and Xgeva together were about six and a half billion dollars in twenty twenty-five, and biosimilars are already launched. Enbrel's decline is accelerating under its negotiated Medicare price. Otezla faces generics around twenty twenty-eight. The growth story leans heavily on MariTide succeeding in Phase three against faster-moving rivals, and its Phase two run required a redesign to manage stomach-related side effects. Roughly three-quarters of product sales come from the United States, maximizing exposure to drug-pricing reform. The tax authorities are pursuing Amgen for about ten point seven billion dollars over transfer pricing tied to Puerto Rico manufacturing. And the balance sheet still carries elevated debt from Horizon.

The way to remember Amgen: it is biotechnology's establishment company, now on its third reinvention. The anemia franchise built it and then cracked; denosumab and Enbrel rebuilt it and are now expiring; the third act is supposed to be rare disease from Horizon plus a monthly obesity shot. Amgen has managed decline before with discipline — dividends, biosimilars, bolt-on deals — but MariTide is the first time in a generation it has swung for the industry's biggest market against its strongest competitors.
