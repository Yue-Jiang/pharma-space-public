Novo Nordisk. The Danish company that turned a gut hormone into the biggest drug franchise in the world — and then discovered how uncomfortable it is to sit on top.

Start with the basics. Novo Nordisk is headquartered in Bagsværd, just outside Copenhagen. Its roots go back to 1923, when Danish scientists began producing insulin, and the modern company was formed in 1989 when two rival Danish insulin makers, Novo and Nordisk, merged. It is controlled by the Novo Nordisk Foundation, a charitable foundation, through a holding company — an ownership structure that matters, as we'll see. In fiscal year 2025 the company booked about three hundred nine billion Danish kroner in sales, roughly forty-six billion dollars, up ten percent at constant exchange rates. It employed around seventy-eight thousand people in mid twenty twenty-five, a number now shrinking by about nine thousand under a major restructuring. Since August of twenty twenty-five the chief executive is Maziar Mike Doustdar, a company veteran who previously ran all of its markets outside the United States.

One molecule dominates everything: semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist — GLP-1 for short — sold under three brand names. Ozempic, the once-weekly injection for type 2 diabetes, did about nineteen billion dollars in twenty twenty-five, making it one of the best-selling drugs on earth. Wegovy, the same molecule at a higher dose for obesity, did nearly twelve billion dollars — and since January twenty twenty-six it also exists as a once-daily pill, the first oral GLP-1 ever approved for weight loss. Rybelsus, the original oral semaglutide tablet for diabetes, added over three billion dollars. Together, semaglutide is roughly three quarters of company revenue. Behind it sits the legacy business: an insulin franchise — including Tresiba, a long-acting insulin — worth about eight billion dollars a year; the older daily GLP-1 liraglutide, sold as Victoza for diabetes and Saxenda for obesity, now fading fast because it's off patent; and a rare disease unit built on NovoSeven for hemophilia and growth hormone products like Norditropin and the newer once-weekly Sogroya.

Now the story. Through the nineteen nineties and two thousands, Novo Nordisk was the world's insulin specialist, converting patients to patented insulin analogs. But the pivotal work happened in its labs in the mid-nineties, where scientist Lotte Bjerre Knudsen figured out how to stabilize the gut hormone GLP-1 into a usable daily drug. That became liraglutide, approved as Victoza in twenty ten — the company's first blockbuster beyond insulin. In twenty fourteen, a higher dose of liraglutide called Saxenda became the first GLP-1 approved for obesity, planting a flag in a market almost nobody believed in. There were stumbles: the next-generation insulin Tresiba was rejected by American regulators in twenty thirteen over heart-safety questions and took until twenty fifteen to reach the United States. And in twenty sixteen, American pricing pressure on insulin forced a profit warning, job cuts, and a change of chief executive — an early lesson in political risk that never really went away. Insulin pricing hearings, lawsuits, and eventually thirty-five-dollar co-pay caps followed.

Then semaglutide changed everything. Ozempic was approved in the United States in December twenty seventeen. Rybelsus, the first oral GLP-1, followed in twenty nineteen. And in June twenty twenty-one came Wegovy, delivering around fifteen percent weight loss — demand so intense the company couldn't make enough of it for years. A landmark trial called SELECT then showed Wegovy cut major cardiovascular events by twenty percent in people with obesity, transforming weight-loss drugs into serious cardiometabolic medicine. Novo Nordisk became Europe's most valuable listed company, worth over six hundred billion dollars at the peak in twenty twenty-four. It spent accordingly: its parent foundation bought the contract manufacturer Catalent for sixteen and a half billion dollars, with Novo Nordisk taking three filling factories for eleven billion, and the company signed a string of acquisitions — Dicerna in RNA interference, Forma in sickle cell disease, Cardior in heart failure.

But twenty twenty-four and twenty twenty-five brought the reckoning. Eli Lilly's rival drug tirzepatide — Mounjaro and Zepbound — proved more effective at driving weight loss. Novo's next great hope, CagriSema, a combination of semaglutide with the amylin analog cagrilintide, delivered about twenty percent weight loss in its pivotal trial in December twenty twenty-four — impressive, but below the twenty-five percent the company had promised, and the stock collapsed. Compounded copies of semaglutide spread across America. After three guidance cuts in twenty twenty-five and a share price down nearly half, the board pushed out chief executive Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen. Mike Doustdar took over in August, cut nine thousand jobs, and then lost a ten-billion-dollar bidding war with Pfizer for the obesity biotech Metsera. In October, a governance clash with the controlling foundation triggered the resignation of the chairman and several directors. Yet the year ended with real wins: Wegovy was approved for the liver disease MASH — metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis; the Wegovy pill was approved in December; CagriSema was filed with the American regulator; and Novo bought Akero Therapeutics, a liver-disease specialist, for about five billion dollars.

So what are the bets now? First, defend the franchise with price. Novo struck a deal with the American administration: cash-paying patients now get Ozempic or Wegovy for three hundred forty-nine dollars a month, and government programs pay around two hundred forty-five. The company is deliberately trading price for volume — which is why it guides to a sales decline of five to thirteen percent in twenty twenty-six. Second, next-generation obesity drugs. CagriSema awaits an approval decision in late twenty twenty-six. Amycretin, a single molecule hitting both the GLP-1 and amylin receptors, showed about twenty-two percent weight loss in early trials and entered phase 3 in both injectable and oral forms — arguably the company's most important internal asset. And from China, Novo licensed UBT251, a triple agonist licensed from United Laboratories for two hundred million dollars upfront, which posted nearly twenty percent weight loss in a mid-stage trial. Third, adjacent diseases: efruxifermin for MASH from the Akero deal, a new hemophilia bispecific antibody called Mim8, and a once-weekly insulin, Awiqli, launching in the United States.

The risks are equally clear. Nearly three quarters of revenue rides on one molecule whose patents have already expired in Canada, China, India, and Brazil as of early twenty twenty-six — with generic makers swarming — and which loses protection in Europe and the United States around twenty thirty-one and twenty thirty-two. Lilly's Zepbound has overtaken Wegovy in American obesity prescriptions, and Lilly's oral drug orforglipron is arriving. American pricing pressure is structural, from Medicare negotiation onward. And the pipeline has already disappointed once; amycretin data are years away.

The through-line of the Novo Nordisk story: a hundred-year focus on metabolic disease produced the defining drug of the decade — and the company's future depends on proving that semaglutide was the beginning of a franchise, not the peak of one.
