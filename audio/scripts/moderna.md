Today's company is Moderna — the messenger RNA pioneer that lived through one of the most dramatic boom and bust cycles in pharmaceutical history, and is now trying to prove it was always more than a one-virus company.

Start with the basics. Moderna is headquartered in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and was founded in September twenty ten as ModeRNA Therapeutics — the name is a play on "modified RNA." It was built by the venture firm Flagship Pioneering around the work of stem cell biologist Derrick Rossi, with co-founders including the M I T inventor Robert Langer and Flagship's Noubar Afeyan, who still chairs the board. The scientific foundation came from academic work by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, who showed that chemically modifying messenger RNA lets it slip past the body's alarm systems, so a synthetic message can instruct cells to make any protein you want. Stéphane Bancel joined as chief executive in twenty eleven and has led the company ever since. For twenty twenty-five, Moderna reported total revenue of about one point nine billion dollars, employed roughly forty-eight hundred people, and posted a net loss of about two point eight billion dollars.

Through the twenty tens, Moderna was famous mostly for raising money — about two billion dollars in venture capital before it ever went public. Partnerships validated the platform: AstraZeneca paid two hundred forty million dollars upfront in twenty thirteen, and a collaboration with Merck beginning in the mid twenty tens would later become critically important for cancer. In December twenty eighteen, Moderna pulled off what was then the largest biotech initial public offering in history, raising about six hundred four million dollars — still with no approved product.

Then came the pandemic, and everything changed. In January twenty twenty, Moderna designed its coronavirus vaccine within days of the virus's genetic sequence being posted online, partnered with the National Institutes of Health, and received emergency authorization in December twenty twenty. Revenue went from about eight hundred million dollars in twenty twenty to eighteen and a half billion in twenty twenty-one, peaking at nineteen point three billion dollars in twenty twenty-two, when the vaccine — branded Spikevax — earned over eighteen billion on its own and the company made more than eight billion dollars in profit.

The bust was just as dramatic. As the world moved past the pandemic, revenue fell to about six point eight billion dollars in twenty twenty-three, then three point two billion in twenty twenty-four, and finally about one point nine billion in twenty twenty-five — roughly a tenth of the peak, with the losses to match. Moderna's answer has been diversification and deep cost cutting. Operating expenses came down by more than two billion dollars, or thirty percent, in twenty twenty-five alone, and in July of that year the company laid off about ten percent of its workforce, taking headcount below five thousand. It ended twenty twenty-five with about eight point one billion dollars in cash and aims to reach breakeven by twenty twenty-eight.

So what does Moderna actually sell today? Everything is a messenger RNA vaccine wrapped in a fatty delivery bubble called a lipid nanoparticle. First, Spikevax, the original COVID vaccine, generic name elasomeran. Second, m-NEXSPIKE, a next-generation COVID vaccine approved in May twenty twenty-five that uses a smaller dose and is easier to store; it quickly captured about a quarter of American retail COVID vaccinations. Together the COVID franchise brought in about one point eight billion dollars in twenty twenty-five. Third, m-RESVIA, a vaccine against respiratory syncytial virus, approved in twenty twenty-four. Commercially it has struggled badly — just eight million dollars in twenty twenty-five sales, because it launched late into a market that GSK and Pfizer had already captured. And fourth, the newest one: m-FLUSIVA, a seasonal influenza vaccine for adults fifty and older, approved by the Food and Drug Administration in August twenty twenty-six — the first messenger RNA flu vaccine ever. There is also m-COMBRIAX, a combined flu and COVID shot, approved in Europe in early twenty twenty-six. Because these are biological vaccines updated every season, Moderna doesn't face traditional patent cliffs — its problem is shrinking demand and competition, not generic copies.

The most exciting bet in the pipeline is cancer. With Merck, Moderna is developing intismeran autogene, also known as m-RNA-forty-one-fifty-seven, an individualized neoantigen therapy. Here's the idea: doctors sequence a patient's tumor, identify up to thirty-four mutations unique to that cancer, and Moderna manufactures a custom messenger RNA vaccine teaching the immune system to hunt those exact mutations, given together with Merck's immunotherapy pembrolizumab, brand name Keytruda. In melanoma patients after surgery, five-year follow-up data announced in January twenty twenty-six showed the combination cut the risk of the cancer coming back, or death, by forty-nine percent compared with pembrolizumab alone. Then in August twenty twenty-six, the Phase three INTerpath zero-zero-one trial met both recurrence-free-survival and distant-metastasis-free-survival endpoints. That is the first positive Phase three readout for an individualized neoantigen therapy, but important details remain unknown: no Phase three effect sizes were disclosed, overall-survival follow-up continues, and regulators have not approved the therapy. Additional trials are running in non-small cell lung cancer, bladder cancer, and kidney cancer. If the result holds through review and commercial-scale personalized manufacturing, it could open a new field of cancer treatment.

But the platform has taken real punches. In October twenty twenty-five, Moderna's cytomegalovirus vaccine — once talked about as a future blockbuster — failed its phase three trial with efficacy between just six and twenty-three percent, and the congenital program was scrapped. In mid twenty twenty-six the norovirus vaccine missed an early-success checkpoint in its phase three trial. And politics has become a genuine business risk. Then there's the courtroom, where Moderna plays both offense and defense. On offense, it sued Pfizer and BioNTech in twenty twenty-two, claiming their COVID vaccine infringed Moderna's patents on modified RNA chemistry; courts in the United Kingdom and Germany have sided with Moderna, and the fight is now before the U K Supreme Court. On defense, Moderna settled a long-running lawsuit in March twenty twenty-six with Arbutus Biopharma and Genevant Sciences, who claimed Moderna used their lipid nanoparticle delivery technology without permission. Moderna agreed to pay nine hundred fifty million dollars upfront, with up to one point three billion more depending on an appeal — potentially the largest patent settlement in pharmaceutical history. A new suit from CureVac arrived in April twenty twenty-six.

So how should you think about Moderna? It's a company with a genuinely revolutionary platform, a proven ability to move at unprecedented speed, and four approved products where two years ago it had one. But it's also burning cash, hostage to a shrinking COVID market, facing a government openly skeptical of its core technology, and betting heavily on cancer data that hasn't fully read out. The next two years — the personalized cancer therapy results, the flu and combination vaccine launches, and the march toward breakeven in twenty twenty-eight — will decide whether Moderna becomes a durable, diversified vaccine and therapeutics company, or remains the greatest one-product story ever told in biotech.
