# Roche — audio deep-dive

Roche is the quiet giant of the pharmaceutical industry. Headquartered in Basel, Switzerland, and founded in eighteen ninety-six by Fritz Hoffmann-La Roche, it is today the world's largest biotech company by revenue and, uniquely among big pharma, also the world leader in diagnostics — the lab tests and analyzers that hospitals run every day. In twenty twenty-five, Roche generated about sixty-one and a half billion Swiss francs in revenue, roughly seventy-four billion US dollars. About forty-eight billion francs came from pharmaceuticals and close to fourteen billion from diagnostics. The company employs around one hundred and three thousand people, and has been led since March twenty twenty-three by chief executive Thomas Schinecker, who previously ran the diagnostics division. Two structural facts define Roche: it fully owns Genentech, the pioneering biotech in South San Francisco that serves as its innovation engine, and it holds a majority stake in the Japanese drugmaker Chugai.

Let's walk through the products that pay the bills. The biggest is Ocrevus, generic name ocrelizumab, an antibody for multiple sclerosis that depletes the immune system's B cells. It sold about seven billion Swiss francs in twenty twenty-five, and it treats both the relapsing and the primary progressive forms of the disease — the latter being an indication no one else had cracked. Second is Hemlibra, or emicizumab, an engineered bispecific antibody for hemophilia A that mimics the missing clotting factor eight; it brought in nearly five billion francs. Third is Vabysmo, or faricimab, an eye medicine for wet age-related macular degeneration and diabetic macular edema, injected into the eye but lasting longer between doses than older drugs; it reached roughly four billion francs just three years after launch. Then comes Tecentriq, or atezolizumab, Roche's cancer immunotherapy, at about three and a half billion francs. And there's a remarkable second act: Xolair, or omalizumab, an anti-allergy antibody more than twenty years old, grew over thirty percent to about three billion francs of US sales after becoming the first medicine approved for food allergies. Rounding out the picture are the breast cancer franchise — Perjeta, generic pertuzumab, and its successor Phesgo, a fixed-dose injection combining pertuzumab and trastuzumab under the skin — plus Polivy for lymphoma, Evrysdi for spinal muscular atrophy, and Alecensa for a genetically defined form of lung cancer.

Now the history, which is one of the great strategic stories in the industry. Roche made its name in the twentieth century with synthetic vitamins and with Valium, the best-selling tranquilizer of its era. But the modern company was built on a nineteen ninety decision to buy sixty percent of Genentech for about two billion dollars — arguably the best acquisition in pharmaceutical history. Through the nineties and two thousands, Genentech invented the antibodies that defined modern cancer treatment: Rituxan for lymphoma in nineteen ninety-seven, Herceptin for breast cancer in nineteen ninety-eight, and Avastin, which starves tumors of blood supply, in two thousand four. Meanwhile Roche paid eleven billion dollars in nineteen ninety-seven for Boehringer Mannheim, making it the world's largest diagnostics company. There was a dark chapter too: in nineteen ninety-nine Roche pleaded guilty to leading a global price-fixing cartel in vitamins and paid a then-record five hundred million dollar fine in the United States; it sold the whole vitamins business in two thousand three. The influenza drug Tamiflu made Roche famous during pandemic scares, though it later drew criticism over withheld trial data.

In March two thousand nine, Roche paid nearly forty-seven billion dollars to buy the rest of Genentech — its biggest deal ever — while carefully preserving Genentech's independent research culture. The strategy became what Roche calls personalized healthcare: pairing targeted drugs with diagnostic tests that identify exactly which patients will benefit.

Then came the cliff. Between twenty seventeen and twenty nineteen, Rituxan, Herceptin and Avastin — which together had peaked at roughly twenty-one billion francs a year — began losing patent protection to biosimilar copies. Losing that much revenue has broken other companies. Roche's answer was one of the most successful launch waves ever: Ocrevus in twenty seventeen, Hemlibra soon after, then Polivy, Evrysdi and Vabysmo. By twenty twenty-five, the five newer growth drivers were generating over twenty-one billion francs — effectively replacing the entire lost franchise. Not everything worked: the Alzheimer's antibody gantenerumab failed in twenty twenty-two, a Huntington's disease program was halted, and a much-hyped cancer immunotherapy target called TIGIT disappointed repeatedly.

Under Thomas Schinecker, Roche has been buying its way into new fields. In late twenty twenty-three it paid seven point one billion dollars for Telavant, gaining a promising antibody for inflammatory bowel disease, and two point seven billion dollars for Carmot Therapeutics, acquiring weight-loss drug candidates. In March twenty twenty-five it struck a major partnership with Denmark's Zealand Pharma — one point six five billion dollars upfront, up to five point three billion in total — for petrelintide, an amylin-based weight-loss drug designed to be gentler on the stomach than current options. In September twenty twenty-five it agreed to buy eighty-nine bio for up to three and a half billion dollars, adding a liver disease drug for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, the serious fatty liver condition. And it has become a repeat customer for Chinese biotech innovation, licensing two antibody-drug conjugates — guided missiles that deliver chemotherapy directly to tumor cells — from Suzhou-based MediLink Therapeutics, most recently in January twenty twenty-six for five hundred seventy million dollars upfront and near-term.

The pipeline now has three headline bets. First, multiple sclerosis: fenebrutinib, an oral brain-penetrant enzyme blocker — a Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitor — delivered three positive Phase Three trials in both relapsing and primary progressive disease, and Roche plans regulatory filings in mid twenty twenty-six. It could become the first high-efficacy pill for the disease, though regulators will scrutinize an imbalance in deaths seen across the trials. Second, Alzheimer's disease: trontinemab uses Roche's brain shuttle technology to ferry an amyloid-clearing antibody across the blood-brain barrier, clearing plaques faster and with less brain swelling than existing drugs in early studies; Phase Three results are expected around twenty twenty-seven. Third, obesity: enicepatide, a dual-hormone injection from the Carmot deal, entered Phase Three in early twenty twenty-six, alongside petrelintide — a crowded race where Roche trails Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk but is betting on better tolerability and combinations.

The risks are concrete. Ocrevus, seven billion francs of revenue, loses patent protection in Europe around twenty twenty-eight and in the United States around twenty twenty-nine. Perjeta faces European biosimilars from twenty twenty-six, and Xolair copies are expected in the United States in late twenty twenty-six. That is why the fenebrutinib and trontinemab readouts matter so much. Roche's infusion-heavy portfolio is also exposed as US Medicare price negotiation expands later this decade, and as tariff threats loom — one reason the company pledged fifty billion dollars of American investment over five years. And the obesity bet, with billions already committed, still has to prove itself in Phase Three.

Step back, and Roche is a study in patient capital: a family-influenced Swiss company that bought the best biotech in the world, let it run, survived the largest patent cliff in industry history by out-inventing it, and is now spending heavily to do it again — this time in the brain, the metabolism, and the immune system. Whether fenebrutinib, trontinemab and the obesity franchise can replace Ocrevus the way Ocrevus replaced Herceptin is the question that will define Roche's next decade.
