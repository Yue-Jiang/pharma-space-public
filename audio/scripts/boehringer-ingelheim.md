Boehringer Ingelheim is the biggest pharmaceutical company most people have never thought about — because you can't buy its stock. Founded in 1885 by Albert Boehringer in the small German town of Ingelheim am Rhein, it remains entirely owned by the Boehringer, Liebrecht, and von Baumbach families. That makes it the largest private drugmaker in the world. In twenty twenty-five, the group booked about twenty-eight billion euros in net sales — roughly thirty-one billion dollars — with about fifty-four thousand employees. Around twenty-three billion euros came from human medicines and nearly five billion from animal health, where Boehringer is the world's number two player. And here is the number that best captures its culture: the company plowed six point four billion euros back into research — nearly twenty-three percent of sales, one of the highest ratios in the entire industry. No quarterly earnings calls, no activist investors. The family motto, in effect, is patience.

The modern story starts in the nineteen nineties and two thousands, when Boehringer built a strong primary-care business: Micardis for blood pressure, Mirapex for Parkinson's disease, Flomax for prostate symptoms. The breakthrough was Spiriva, launched in two thousand two with Pfizer — a once-daily inhaler for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease that became the world's leading treatment for that condition and the company's first multi-billion blockbuster.

Then came a harder lesson. In twenty ten, the FDA approved Pradaxa, known generically as dabigatran — the first of a new generation of oral blood thinners designed to replace warfarin for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. Sales took off, but the drug initially had no antidote, and patients who suffered serious bleeding had no way to reverse it. Lawsuits piled up, and in twenty fourteen Boehringer paid six hundred fifty million dollars to settle roughly four thousand American cases. Characteristically, the company answered with science: one year later it won approval for Praxbind, a specific reversal agent for its own blood thinner.

The same era produced the company's two modern pillars. A diabetes alliance signed with Eli Lilly in twenty eleven delivered Trajenta, and then, in twenty fourteen, Jardiance — generic name empagliflozin — a pill that helps the kidneys excrete excess sugar. A landmark trial in twenty fifteen showed Jardiance actually reduced cardiovascular death, the first diabetes drug ever to do so. That discovery turned it from a diabetes pill into a heart and kidney medicine, with approvals in heart failure and chronic kidney disease. By twenty twenty-five, Jardiance was bringing in eight point eight billion euros a year — with the wider franchise topping ten billion dollars. Also in twenty fourteen came Ofev, generic name nintedanib, for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — a devastating scarring disease of the lungs. Ofev made Boehringer the world leader in pulmonary fibrosis and now sells about three point eight billion euros annually.

The boldest structural move came in twenty sixteen and seventeen: a giant asset swap with Sanofi of France, worth about twenty-five billion dollars in total. Boehringer handed over its consumer health business — brands like the laxative Dulcolax — plus four point seven billion euros in cash, and received Merial, Sanofi's animal health division, home of the flea and tick treatment NexGard and the heartworm brand Heartgard. Overnight, Boehringer became the world's second-largest animal health company. NexGard alone now sells one point four billion euros a year.

Recent years have been about rebuilding the pipeline before the old blockbusters fade. The company bought antibody-drug-conjugate specialist NBE Therapeutics, and T3 Pharmaceuticals, a Swiss firm using bacteria to deliver cancer therapies. It licensed survodutide — more on that in a moment — from Zealand Pharma of Denmark. Not everything worked: in January twenty twenty-five, a schizophrenia drug called iclepertin failed all its goals in a massive Phase three program, ending hopes for the first treatment for cognitive impairment in schizophrenia.

But twenty twenty-five overall was a triumph. In August, the FDA approved Hernexeos — generic name zongertinib — the first oral targeted pill for a form of advanced non-small cell lung cancer driven by mutations in a gene called HER2. In October came Jascayd, generic name nerandomilast, the first genuinely new medicine for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in more than a decade, approved in both the United States and China, and positioned as the successor to Ofev. Leadership changed generations too: Christian Boehringer stepped down after eighteen years chairing the family shareholders' committee, and Shashank Deshpande — a manager who rose through the American, Japanese, and German businesses — became chairman of the board of managing directors, the company's chief-executive role.

So what are the bets now? The biggest is survodutide, a weekly injection that activates two gut-hormone receptors — glucagon and G L P one. In Phase three results announced in April twenty twenty-six, patients lost about seventeen percent of their body weight. That trails Eli Lilly's best numbers, but survodutide showed something intriguing: it preserved more muscle and cut liver fat by up to sixty-three percent. Boehringer is aiming it not just at obesity but at metabolic liver disease — the scarring liver condition known as MASH — where two huge Phase three trials are underway. A next-generation triple-hormone drug enters mid-stage trials in twenty twenty-six. In cancer, zongertinib is being tested as a first-choice treatment, where it shrank tumors in seventy-seven percent of previously untreated patients. And like much of the industry, Boehringer is shopping in China: in January twenty twenty-six it licensed an experimental antibody for inflammatory bowel disease from Shanghai's Simcere in a deal worth up to about one billion euros.

The risks are real. Jardiance and Ofev together are more than half of human-pharma revenue, and both face the end of patent protection around the end of this decade — with Jardiance also subject to a negotiated Medicare price in America starting in twenty twenty-six. The company's answer is a promised twenty-five product launches over the next ten years. Ambitious — but this is a hundred-forty-year-old family firm that thinks in generations, not quarters. Few companies are better built to wait out a storm.
