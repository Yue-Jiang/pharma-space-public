Boehringer Ingelheim is the large pharmaceutical company many investors rarely discuss because its shares are not for sale. Founded in eighteen eighty-five by Albert Boehringer in Ingelheim am Rhein, Germany, it remains controlled by the Boehringer, Liebrecht, and von Baumbach families. That private ownership shapes its unusually long time horizon.

In twenty twenty-five, Boehringer generated about twenty-seven point eight billion euros in group net sales, with roughly twenty-two point seven billion from human medicines and nearly five billion from animal health. It employed about fifty-four thousand people. Shashank Deshpande became chairman of the board of managing directors, effectively the chief executive role, in July twenty twenty-five. The most revealing number may be research spending: about six point four billion euros, or nearly twenty-three percent of sales. Because the company is private, it discloses less product-level financial detail than listed peers, but it can invest without managing quarterly market expectations.

The modern story began with a strong primary-care portfolio. Through the nineteen nineties and early two thousands, Boehringer sold Micardis for high blood pressure, Mirapex for Parkinson's disease, and Flomax for prostate symptoms. Its first major global blockbuster was Spiriva, generic name tiotropium, a once-daily inhaled bronchodilator launched in two thousand two. Spiriva became the world's leading medicine for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and still produced roughly one billion euros in twenty twenty-five, although generic competition is driving a long decline.

Pradaxa, generic name dabigatran etexilate, brought both scientific progress and legal trouble. Approved in the United States in twenty ten, it was the first of a new generation of oral blood thinners to challenge warfarin for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. Sales rose quickly, but serious bleeding became a focus because the drug initially had no specific antidote. In twenty fourteen, Boehringer paid six hundred fifty million dollars to settle roughly four thousand American lawsuits. The company answered the clinical problem directly with Praxbind, generic name idarucizumab, a reversal agent approved in twenty fifteen.

The same period created Boehringer's two current pillars. An alliance with Eli Lilly produced Trajenta, generic name linagliptin, and then Jardiance, generic name empagliflozin. Jardiance began as a diabetes medicine that helps the kidneys remove glucose. A landmark cardiovascular outcomes trial in twenty fifteen showed that it reduced cardiovascular death. Later approvals in heart failure and chronic kidney disease turned it into a broad cardio-renal-metabolic franchise. It generated about eight point eight billion euros for Boehringer in twenty twenty-five, with combinations taking the wider franchise above ten billion dollars.

The second pillar is Ofev, generic name nintedanib, approved in twenty fourteen for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. It later expanded into other progressive fibrosing lung diseases and produced about three point eight billion euros in twenty twenty-five. Together, Jardiance and Ofev account for more than half of human-pharma sales, which explains both the company's strength and its urgency as their patent protection approaches the end of the decade.

In twenty sixteen and twenty seventeen, Boehringer reshaped itself through a giant swap with Sanofi. It transferred its consumer health business, valued at about six point seven billion euros, and paid about four point seven billion euros in cash. In return, it received Sanofi's Merial animal-health business, valued at about eleven point four billion euros. The transaction, worth roughly twenty-five billion dollars in total, made Boehringer the world's second-largest animal-health company. NexGard, generic name afoxolaner, became a major flea and tick brand and generated about one point four billion euros in twenty twenty-five. Vetmedin, generic name pimobendan, remains another leading animal-health product.

Recent years have centered on rebuilding the pipeline. Boehringer bought N B E Therapeutics for antibody-drug conjugates and T three Pharmaceuticals for bacteria-based cancer delivery. It expanded partnerships in oncology and immune disease. One major bet failed in January twenty twenty-five: the large phase three program for iclepertin missed every primary and secondary endpoint in cognitive impairment associated with schizophrenia.

But twenty twenty-five also delivered two important launches. Hernexeos, generic name zongertinib, became the first oral targeted therapy for previously treated advanced non-small cell lung cancer driven by mutations in human epidermal growth factor receptor two, usually called H E R two. Jascayd, generic name nerandomilast, became the first new medicine for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in more than a decade and soon added progressive pulmonary fibrosis. Jascayd is the direct succession plan for Ofev, while zongertinib establishes a beachhead in precision oncology.

The largest pipeline bet is survodutide, a once-weekly drug licensed from Zealand Pharma. It activates both the glucagon and glucagon-like peptide one receptors. In a phase three obesity study, patients lost an average of about seventeen percent of body weight over seventy-six weeks, compared with about three percent on placebo. Boehringer is a late entrant behind Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk, so it is emphasizing possible differentiation in liver fat and preservation of lean mass. Two phase three trials are testing survodutide in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, a progressive liver disease. A next-generation triple agonist, targeting glucagon-like peptide one, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, and neuropeptide Y two, is in phase two development.

Kidney disease is another attempt to extend the Jardiance franchise. Apecotrep inhibits transient receptor potential canonical six, usually called T R P C six, and is in phase three for focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, a rare scarring kidney disorder with no approved disease-modifying therapy. Vicadrostat blocks aldosterone synthesis and is being tested with Jardiance in chronic kidney disease. In respiratory medicine, verducatib is in phase three for bronchiectasis, a condition that still lacks a medicine approved for the disease itself.

The oncology pipeline is broader than zongertinib. Obrixtamig is a bispecific T-cell engager that connects delta-like ligand three, or D L L three, on cancer cells with cluster of differentiation three, or C D three, on T cells. Two phase three trials began in June twenty twenty-six in small cell lung cancer and other neuroendocrine cancers. Brigimadlin, an oral drug targeting the M D M two and p fifty-three pathway, is in registrational development for a type of liposarcoma. Boehringer has also licensed a preclinical bispecific antibody from China's Simcere for inflammatory bowel disease in a deal worth up to roughly one billion euros.

The risks start with concentration. Jardiance and Ofev together generated about twelve point six billion euros in twenty twenty-five. Jardiance faces a negotiated Medicare price from twenty twenty-six and possible American generic entry around twenty twenty-nine. Ofev also approaches loss of exclusivity near the end of the decade. The newer launches treat smaller populations and may ramp slowly. Survodutide enters a fiercely competitive obesity market, with questions about tolerability and whether its liver profile can create a durable commercial niche. The iclepertin failure also shows how much attrition can sit behind a promise of twenty-five launches over ten years.

Still, Boehringer Ingelheim is structurally built to absorb long development cycles. Its task is clear: use private ownership, unusually heavy research spending, and patience to replace two enormous products before their cliffs arrive. The next few years will show whether Jascayd, Hernexeos, survodutide, kidney medicines, and precision oncology can turn that patience into another generation of growth.
