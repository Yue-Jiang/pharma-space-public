Bayer. The company that invented aspirin, the German conglomerate that bought Monsanto and lived to regret it, and — quietly, underneath all that noise — one of the more interesting pharmaceutical turnaround stories of the mid twenty-twenties.

Let's start with the shape of the company, because Bayer is unusual. It is not a pure pharmaceutical company. It is a three-division conglomerate headquartered in Leverkusen, Germany, founded all the way back in eighteen sixty-three. In twenty twenty-five the group brought in about forty-five point six billion euros in total sales. Crop science — seeds and agricultural chemicals — is actually the biggest division at around twenty-one and a half billion euros. Pharmaceuticals came in at about seventeen point eight billion euros, and consumer health, the aspirin-and-skincare business, at about five point eight billion. The group employs roughly eighty-eight thousand people. The chief executive is Bill Anderson, an American who previously ran Roche's pharma business, and who took over in June of twenty twenty-three. The pharma division is run from Berlin by Stefan Oelrich.

Now the history, because you cannot understand Bayer's present without it.

At the turn of the millennium, Bayer suffered one of the industry's famous safety disasters. In two thousand one it withdrew its cholesterol drug, cerivastatin — sold as Baycol and Lipobay — after the drug was linked to a rare muscle-destroying side effect and roughly one hundred deaths. The litigation cost more than a billion dollars and pushed Bayer out of pharma's top tier.

The rebuild came in two thousand six, when Bayer outbid a rival to buy Berlin-based Schering A G for roughly seventeen billion euros — at the time the biggest acquisition in its history. Schering brought women's health, including the Yasmin birth control pills and the Mirena intrauterine device, plus cancer drugs and the radiology contrast media business. Around the same time, Bayer's own labs produced a genuine blockbuster: rivaroxaban, sold as Xarelto, an oral blood thinner launched in two thousand eight and partnered with Johnson and Johnson in the United States. A few years later Bayer added the rights outside the United States to Eylea, an injectable eye medicine from Regeneron for macular degeneration. Xarelto and Eylea would carry the division for the next fifteen years.

Then came the deal that defines modern Bayer. In twenty sixteen, chief executive Werner Baumann agreed to buy Monsanto, the American seeds giant, for sixty-three billion dollars. The deal closed in June twenty eighteen — and within weeks, a California jury decided that Monsanto's glyphosate weedkiller, Roundup, had caused a groundskeeper's lymphoma. More than one hundred thousand claims piled up. Bayer's market value at times fell below the price it had paid for Monsanto. In twenty twenty the company agreed to pay up to about eleven billion dollars to settle most of the claims, and it sold its animal health business to Elanco for seven point six billion dollars to shore up the balance sheet. Litigation has dogged the company ever since — but there was a genuine turning point recently, and we'll come back to it.

While all that was happening, the pharma division was quietly rebuilding its pipeline. It bought BlueRock Therapeutics for stem cell therapies, AskBio for gene therapy — a deal worth up to four billion dollars — a British biotech called KaNDy Therapeutics for a menopause drug, and Vividion Therapeutics for a billion and a half dollars for its drug discovery platform. And two homegrown or partnered products launched that would become the new growth engines: Nubeqa, generic name darolutamide, a prostate cancer pill developed with Finland's Orion; and Kerendia, generic name finerenone, for chronic kidney disease in people with type two diabetes.

Bill Anderson arrived as chief executive in June twenty twenty-three, and almost immediately took a punch: the company's great cardiovascular hope, a next-generation blood thinner called asundexian, failed its big atrial fibrillation trial that November, and the stock hit multi-decade lows. Anderson responded by tearing up the org chart — a program he calls dynamic shared ownership, which stripped out management layers and thousands of jobs — cutting the dividend by about ninety-five percent to the legal minimum, and deciding, after a review, to keep the conglomerate together rather than break it up.

And here is where the story gets interesting, because the turnaround is actually visible in the numbers. Bayer's two legacy blockbusters are in structural decline. Xarelto lost patent protection in Europe and elsewhere: its sales fell from over four billion euros in twenty twenty-three to about two point three billion in twenty twenty-five, down a third in a single year, with another decline of up to forty percent expected in twenty twenty-six. Eylea, at about three point one billion euros, is now being squeezed by biosimilar copies. That is a brutal double patent cliff.

But the new products are covering the gap. Nubeqa grew fifty-seven percent in twenty twenty-five to about two point four billion euros, making it Bayer's top-selling pharmaceutical by early twenty twenty-six. Kerendia grew seventy-nine percent to about eight hundred thirty million euros, and won an important new approval in the United States in July twenty twenty-five for a common form of heart failure. Together those two drugs contributed over three billion euros. And twenty twenty-five brought three new product launches: Beyonttra, generic name acoramidis, a heart medicine for transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy licensed from BridgeBio for European markets; Lynkuet, generic name elinzanetant, a first-of-its-kind non-hormonal pill for menopausal hot flashes, approved in the United States in October; and Hyrnuo, generic name sevabertinib, a targeted pill for lung cancer with a specific HER2 mutation, granted accelerated approval in November.

Even the asundexian story has a second act. In late twenty twenty-five the drug succeeded in a different setting — preventing second strokes in people who have already had one — becoming the first drug of its class to complete a positive phase three trial. Bayer believes it has blockbuster potential and plans regulatory filings.

The pipeline behind that includes some bold modality bets: a stem-cell-derived therapy for Parkinson's disease called bemdaneprocel, now in phase three; a one-time gene therapy for heart failure from AskBio in phase two; and a new generation M R I contrast agent. Bayer is also shopping in China, licensing a novel oral cancer drug from Puhe BioPharma in twenty twenty-five and running a biotech incubator in Shanghai.

And the litigation? February twenty twenty-six brought a proposed seven point two five billion dollar class settlement to resolve current and future Roundup claims. Then, on June twenty-fifth, twenty twenty-six, Monsanto won its landmark Roundup case at the United States Supreme Court, a ruling that limits the state-law cancer suits at the heart of the litigation. It was the win the company had chased for years, and it may finally cap the damage.

So what should you remember? Bayer is a conglomerate whose pharma division just executed a rare feat: absorbing the simultaneous loss of its two biggest drugs while returning the portfolio to growth, powered by a prostate cancer pill and a kidney drug. The risks are real — about five and a half billion euros of revenue still in decline, heavy debt, a dividend at the legal minimum, and growth concentrated in just a few products. But after nearly a decade defined by a disastrous acquisition, the company enters twenty twenty-six with its strongest pharmaceutical portfolio in years, and — for the first time since twenty eighteen — a credible end to the Roundup saga.
