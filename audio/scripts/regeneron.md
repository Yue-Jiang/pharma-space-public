Regeneron Pharmaceuticals is one of the great science-first stories in biotechnology — a company run for nearly four decades by the same two physician-scientists, and a company whose fortunes today hinge on a dramatic handoff from one blockbuster to another.

Start with the basics. Regeneron is headquartered in Tarrytown, New York, just north of New York City. It was founded in nineteen eighty-eight by Leonard Schleifer, a neurologist, who recruited a brilliant young Columbia scientist named George Yancopoulos the following year. Schleifer remains chief executive officer and Yancopoulos remains chief scientific officer — the longest-running founder partnership at the top of any major American drug company. In twenty twenty-five, Regeneron reported about fourteen point three billion dollars in revenue and employed roughly fifteen thousand four hundred people. But that revenue number understates the company's real commercial footprint, for reasons we'll get to.

The name Regeneron reflects its original mission: regenerating nerves. That mission failed. Through the nineteen nineties and early two thousands, the company's nerve-growth-factor programs for diseases like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis collapsed in clinical trials, and an obesity drug called Axokine failed in two thousand three. What saved Regeneron was what it built along the way: technology platforms. The most important is called VelocImmune — genetically engineered mice with humanized immune systems that produce fully human antibodies, quickly and reliably. Nearly every drug Regeneron has ever brought to market came from its own labs, which is genuinely unusual in this industry. The company's first approved product, Arcalyst, for rare inflammatory syndromes, arrived only in two thousand eight — twenty years after founding.

Then came the drug that changed everything. Eylea, known generically as aflibercept, is a protein injected directly into the eye that traps a molecule called vascular endothelial growth factor. Approved in twenty eleven for wet age-related macular degeneration — a leading cause of blindness in the elderly — Eylea became a colossus, at its peak generating more than nine billion dollars a year globally, with Bayer selling it outside the United States. For a decade, Eylea essentially was Regeneron.

The second pillar came through a partnership with the French pharmaceutical giant Sanofi. That alliance produced several drugs, but one towers over the rest: Dupixent, generic name dupilumab, an antibody that blocks two inflammatory signals called interleukin four and interleukin thirteen. First approved in twenty seventeen for eczema, Dupixent kept adding diseases — asthma, nasal polyps, a swallowing disorder called eosinophilic esophagitis, chronic hives, and in twenty twenty-four it became the first biologic medicine ever approved for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. In twenty twenty-five, Dupixent's global sales reached about seventeen point eight billion dollars, growing twenty-six percent. Here's the accounting quirk: Sanofi records those sales, and Regeneron takes roughly half the profits as collaboration revenue. So Regeneron's income statement looks smaller than its actual franchise.

The partnership story hasn't all been smooth. Praluent, a cholesterol-lowering antibody launched in twenty fifteen, was expected to be a multi-billion-dollar seller but flopped against insurance-company resistance — and it dragged Regeneron and Sanofi into a decade of patent warfare with Amgen, which Regeneron's side ultimately won at the United States Supreme Court in twenty twenty-three.

During the pandemic, Regeneron had a moment of fame: its antibody cocktail against COVID-nineteen, called REGEN-COV, was given to President Trump in October twenty twenty and drove company revenue to a peak of about sixteen point one billion dollars in twenty twenty-one, before the Omicron variant made it obsolete. The company used that windfall wisely — buying back full rights to its cancer immunotherapy Libtayo from Sanofi for nine hundred million dollars upfront, and acquiring small companies in cancer and gene therapy for hearing loss.

Which brings us to the central drama of today: the Eylea patent cliff. Copycat biosimilar versions of aflibercept began arriving in late twenty twenty-four, when Amgen launched its version after Regeneron failed to block it in court. Regeneron then settled with a queue of other biosimilar makers — Sandoz, Formycon, Celltrion, Alvotech and Teva — clearing them to launch from late twenty twenty-six. Add fierce competition from Roche's rival drug Vabysmo and cheap compounded alternatives, and United States sales of the Eylea franchise fell twenty-seven percent in twenty twenty-five, to about four point four billion dollars. Regeneron's defense is Eylea H D, a high-dose version approved in twenty twenty-three that lets patients go longer between eye injections. It grew thirty-six percent in twenty twenty-five to about one point six billion dollars, and won new approvals for retinal vein occlusion and monthly dosing. But the defense has been hampered by an embarrassing manufacturing saga: the F D A repeatedly rejected a prefilled syringe version because of problems at a contract factory in Indiana owned by Novo Nordisk.

So what are the bets for the future? Five stand out. First, obesity — with a twist. Rather than chase the market leaders head-on, Regeneron is focused on what it calls quality of weight loss: preserving muscle while losing fat. It's testing trevogrumab, an antibody that blocks the muscle-limiting protein myostatin, on top of semaglutide. And in June twenty twenty-five it licensed a Chinese-originated drug from Hansoh Pharma — olatorepatide, a dual incretin agonist similar to Eli Lilly's Zepbound — for eighty million dollars upfront and up to about one point nine billion in milestones. That drug showed roughly nineteen percent weight loss in a Chinese phase three trial, and global trials are planned.

Second, genetic medicines. Regeneron runs one of the world's largest human genetics operations, having sequenced nearly three million volunteers, and it even tried to buy the bankrupt consumer genetics company Twenty-three and Me in twenty twenty-five — winning the auction at two hundred fifty-six million dollars before being outbid. Its gene therapy for children born deaf due to otoferlin gene mutations, called D B dash O T O, has restored hearing in early trials and awaits an F D A decision in the first half of twenty twenty-six. There are also partnered programs using gene silencing with Alnylam and gene editing with Intellia.

Third, rare disease. An antibody called garetosmab prevented more than ninety-nine percent of abnormal bone growth in a devastating condition called fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva, where muscle gradually turns to bone. It's now under priority review.

Fourth, cancer. Beyond Libtayo, which passed one and a half billion dollars in twenty twenty-five sales, Regeneron is building a family of bispecific antibodies — drugs that grab a cancer cell with one arm and a killer T cell with the other — including the newly approved Lynozyfic for multiple myeloma.

And fifth, respiratory disease, where itepekimab, a follow-on to Dupixent for former smokers with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, delivered one successful and one failed phase three trial in twenty twenty-five — a reminder that even great research engines misfire.

The risks are clear. Original Eylea revenue is melting away now, and the biosimilar wave crests in late twenty twenty-six. The company's profits are increasingly concentrated in a single drug, Dupixent, whose sales are booked by a partner. Manufacturing stumbles have cost real market share. And American drug-pricing politics loom over both major franchises.

Still, Regeneron enters twenty twenty-six with real momentum: revenue growing double digits again by mid twenty twenty-six, about fifty drug candidates in the clinic, nearly six billion dollars a year spent on research, nine billion dollars committed to new American manufacturing, and — for the first time in its history — a dividend. For a company built by two scientists who spent twenty years without a hit product, patience remains the defining virtue.
