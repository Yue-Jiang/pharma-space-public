Merck and Company — known as M-S-D, or Merck Sharp and Dohme, outside the United States and Canada — is one of the largest pharmaceutical companies in the world. Headquartered in Rahway, New Jersey, it was founded in 1891 as the American subsidiary of the German Merck family business, and became fully independent in 1917. It should not be confused with the German Merck, which still exists as a separate company. Today's Merck employs about seventy-five thousand people and reported revenue of about sixty-five billion dollars in fiscal year 2025. Its chief executive is Robert Davis, and its focus areas are cancer medicine, vaccines, heart and lung disease, infectious disease, and animal health.

One product towers over everything else. Keytruda, generic name pembrolizumab, is an antibody that blocks a protein called P-D-1 on immune cells, releasing the brakes on the immune system so it can attack tumors. It is approved in roughly forty cancer indications — lung cancer, melanoma, head and neck cancer, and many others — and in 2025 it generated thirty-one point seven billion dollars, making it the best-selling branded drug in the world and roughly half of Merck's entire revenue. In late 2025 Merck launched Keytruda Q-lex, an under-the-skin injection that takes a minute or two instead of a half-hour infusion — a key defensive move, because the original intravenous Keytruda loses United States patent protection in 2028.

The rest of the portfolio is a supporting cast. Gardasil, the human papillomavirus vaccine that prevents cervical and other cancers, was once a nine billion dollar franchise but fell thirty-nine percent in 2025 to about five point two billion dollars, dragged down by collapsing demand in China. Winrevair, generic name sotatercept, a biologic for pulmonary arterial hypertension acquired with Acceleron, reached one point four billion dollars in its second year. Bridion, generic sugammadex, which reverses muscle relaxants after surgery, sells roughly one point eight billion dollars a year but loses American exclusivity in 2026 — as do the diabetes pills Januvia and Janumet, generic name sitagliptin, worth together about two point seven billion dollars. And Ohtuvayre, generic ensifentrine, a newly acquired inhaled treatment for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, is in the middle of one of the strongest respiratory drug launches in history.

Merck's modern history begins with a fall from grace. In the 1990s it was the industry's most admired science company, built on cholesterol drug Zocor, bone drug Fosamax, and asthma pill Singulair. But in September 2004 Merck voluntarily withdrew its blockbuster painkiller Vioxx after data showed it raised the risk of heart attacks and strokes. The recall erased about two and a half billion dollars in annual sales, brought tens of thousands of lawsuits, and cost four point eight five billion dollars in a 2007 settlement, plus almost a billion more to the government later.

The rebuild came in two acts. First, homegrown launches in 2006 and 2007: Gardasil, Januvia, and the H-I-V drug Isentress. Second, the roughly forty-one billion dollar merger with Schering-Plough in 2009. That deal brought allergy and cholesterol franchises — but its greatest prize was hidden in the pipeline: an experimental antibody against P-D-1 that Schering-Plough had inherited when it bought the Dutch company Organon. That molecule became pembrolizumab. Merck initially shelved it, then raced it through development once rival Bristol Myers Squibb validated the approach. Keytruda won its first approval, in melanoma, in September 2014. The decisive insight was to pair it with a diagnostic test measuring the target protein in tumors and go straight at newly diagnosed lung cancer. Landmark trials in 2016 and 2018 made Keytruda the backbone of lung cancer treatment, and it never looked back.

Since 2021, under chief executive Rob Davis, the company has been engineered around one question: what happens when Keytruda's patent expires? Merck spun off its women's health and older products business as Organon in 2021, then spent aggressively. Eleven and a half billion dollars for Acceleron in 2021, which became Winrevair. Ten point eight billion for Prometheus Biosciences in 2023, adding tulisokibart, an antibody for ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease now in phase three. A four billion dollar upfront alliance with Japan's Daiichi Sankyo for antibody-drug conjugates. Ten billion dollars for Verona Pharma in 2025, bringing Ohtuvayre. And just over nine billion for Cidara Therapeutics, whose lead asset is a long-acting antiviral, now in phase three, designed to prevent influenza for a whole season with a single dose.

Notably, Merck has become one of the biggest buyers of Chinese-origin science. Its lead cancer pipeline asset, sacituzumab tirumotecan — an antibody-drug conjugate against the target TROP-2, licensed from Kelun-Biotech — is in numerous phase three trials with positive lung and endometrial cancer results already in hand. A bispecific antibody hitting both P-D-1 and V-E-G-F, licensed from LaNova Medicines for five hundred eighty-eight million dollars upfront, is Merck's hedge against a new drug class that threatens to leapfrog Keytruda. And an oral pill against lipoprotein little-a, a stubborn inherited cholesterol risk factor, was licensed from Jiangsu Hengrui Pharma in 2025.

The internal pipeline is delivering too. Lipfendra, generic name enlicitide, became the first oral P-C-S-K-9 inhibitor approved by the Food and Drug Administration in 2026 — a daily pill that cuts bad cholesterol by more than half, doing what previously required injections. Merck is also developing a personalized messenger-R-N-A cancer vaccine with Moderna, and once-weekly oral H-I-V regimens, including one partnered with Gilead. Management says the pipeline could generate over seventy billion dollars in annual revenue by the mid twenty-thirties, and in 2026 it reorganized into two commercial units — oncology, and specialty medicines with infectious disease — while cutting three billion dollars in annual costs to reinvest.

The risks are concentrated and well known. Keytruda's 2028 patent cliff puts nearly half of revenue in play, and biosimilar competitors are preparing. The 2026 expirations of Januvia, Janumet, and Bridion drain roughly four billion dollars more. Gardasil's China problem looks structural, with local competitors multiplying. Medicare price negotiation under the Inflation Reduction Act has already hit Januvia and could reach Keytruda later this decade. And the pipeline, while broad, leans heavily on oncology and on partnered assets whose data are still maturing.

The investment story, then, is a race: can Winrevair, Ohtuvayre, Capvaxive, the antibody-drug conjugates, the oral cholesterol pill, and the flu antiviral collectively outrun the largest single-product patent cliff in pharmaceutical history? Merck's own framing — a hill, not a cliff — will be tested from 2028 onward.
