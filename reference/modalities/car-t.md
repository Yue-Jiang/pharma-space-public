# CAR-T (chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy)

## What it is
A CAR-T therapy is a living drug made from the patient's own immune cells. T cells are collected from the patient's blood (**leukapheresis**), shipped to a factory, and given a gene — usually via a disabled virus — that makes them display a **chimeric antigen receptor**: an artificial protein whose outer half grips a marker on the cancer cell and whose inner half fires the T cell's kill machinery. The engineered cells are grown, frozen, shipped back, and infused after chemotherapy clears space for them (**lymphodepletion**). Every approved CAR-T aims at one of two markers: **CD19** on B cells (leukemias, lymphomas) or **BCMA** on plasma cells (multiple myeloma).

## Why it matters
CAR-T is the only cancer modality that routinely produces years-long remissions — in some cases with no further treatment — in patients who have exhausted every other option. That is the case for the modality. The case against it is economics: list prices near $400-500K, a manufacturing chain that must be run once per patient, hospital infrastructure requirements that restrict delivery to certified centres, and, consequently, a commercial ceiling around $1-1.5B per product. Only three CAR-Ts have ever cleared $1B a year (Yescarta, Breyanzi, Carvykti), and a large share of medically eligible patients never receive cell therapy at all. The industry's response is not to abandon the mechanism but to change the factory — see the in-vivo pivot below.

**Kymriah is the origin point.** Approved **30 August 2017** for refractory or second-relapse B-cell ALL in patients up to age 25, it was the first CAR-T — and the first gene-modified cell therapy of any kind — cleared by the FDA, adding 3L+ large B-cell lymphoma in May 2018 (JULIET) and later 3L+ follicular lymphoma. It is also the cautionary tale: Q4 sales eroded from $120M (2023) to $108M (2024) to $85M (2025), the last down 21% year on year, which Novartis attributes to competition in most markets.

**The in-vivo pivot is the story of 2025-2026.** Instead of manufacturing cells outside the body, these approaches inject a delivery vehicle — a lipid nanoparticle or engineered virus — that reprogrammes T cells *inside* the patient, deleting apheresis, the factory and the slot queue at once. Big pharma bought in fast: AbbVie/Capstan ($2.1B, closed Aug 2025), BMS/Orbital ($1.5B, 2025), Roche/Poseida (~$1.1B, closed Jan 2025), AstraZeneca/EsoBiotec (~$1B, 2025). Note the indication drift — the lead in-vivo assets target **autoimmune disease**, not cancer. A cheap, transient "immune reset" that clears autoreactive B cells is a far larger market than late-line lymphoma, and only becomes thinkable once manufacturing cost disappears.

## The graveyard
See `reference/05_graveyard.md` for the registry. Three distinct lessons:

- **Molecule-level, same target.** Kymriah missed in second-line lymphoma (reported August 2021) while Yescarta (ZUMA-7) and Breyanzi succeeded in the same setting against the same target. Identical biology, different construct and manufacturing reliability — CD19 was not the variable.
- **Platform-level.** Takeda wound down its entire cell-therapy platform in 2025 after **Alofisel** (a stem-cell therapy for Crohn's fistulas, from the 2018 TiGenix deal) failed Phase 3 in 2023; Vertex discontinued its encapsulated-islet therapy VX-264 in 2025. Cell therapy is a capital-intensive platform bet — when the lead asset dies, the platform often goes with it.
- **Target-class limit.** No CAR-T has been approved in a solid tumour: solid tumours lack a clean universal surface antigen, and their microenvironment suppresses infused T cells. A biology wall, not an execution failure.

Safety is its own attrition risk: all CAR-Ts carry boxed warnings for cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity and secondary T-cell malignancies, and Carvykti's delayed neurotoxicity (including parkinsonism) is the central marketing argument for anito-cel.

## Competitive dynamics

**Myeloma is where the money is, and Carvykti won it.** Its April 2024 US approval for second-line use — the first BCMA therapy available that early — carried it to $1.9B in 2025 (Q4 $555M, +66%), past 10,000 patients treated cumulatively and, by mid-2026, 348 sites in 19 markets; Legend Biotech reached its first profitable quarter on it. Abecma, on the same target, stalled near $0.4B, and BMS bought out partner 2seventy bio for $286M in 2025 essentially to consolidate a decaying asset. Gilead's answer is anito-cel, acquired via the $7.8B Arcellx purchase (closed April 2026) and pitched on a cleaner delayed-neurotoxicity profile, with a 23 December 2026 PDUFA date.

**Lymphoma is a three-way fight shrinking at the edges.** Breyanzi grew 82% to $1.4B in 2025 on relentless label expansion (five B-cell indications, marginal zone lymphoma added December 2025), while Gilead's Yescarta (−5%) and Tecartus (−15%) both fell — roughly −7% for the franchise. The pressure comes from **T-cell engagers** (see `t-cell-engagers.md`): an off-the-shelf antibody given in an outpatient clinic beats a five-week bespoke manufacturing run for many patients, even at lower response depth. Kymriah, the first mover, is the clearest loser of that squeeze.

**Sequencing is now the strategic question in myeloma.** The emerging pattern is CAR-T first (cilta-cel), then a BCMA engager, then a GPRC5D-directed agent once the tumour stops expressing BCMA — **antigen escape**. Hence BMS developing a GPRC5D CAR-T (arlo-cel) and J&J marketing both a BCMA and a GPRC5D engager: the franchise is the sequence, not the asset.

**The next platform war is manufacturing, not targeting.** Autolus's Aucatzyl was approved in November 2024 without a REMS programme — a first for the modality — and Autolus markets a purpose-built facility and short turnaround against an industry norm of 3-6 weeks. In 2025 the FDA approved removal of the REMS programmes from BMS's Breyanzi and Abecma labels with streamlined post-infusion monitoring. Every one of these moves attacks logistics rather than biology; in-vivo CAR-T is the same argument taken to its conclusion.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Autologous / allogeneic | Made from the patient's own cells (all approved CAR-Ts) versus batch-manufactured from a healthy donor, "off-the-shelf" (no approvals yet). |
| Leukapheresis | The blood-filtering procedure that collects the patient's T cells. |
| Lymphodepletion | Chemotherapy before infusion (typically fludarabine + cyclophosphamide) to make room for the CAR-T cells. |
| Vein-to-vein time | Days from cell collection to infusion, typically 3-6 weeks. A meaningful fraction of patients deteriorate or die in that window. |
| Manufacturing slot | A reserved factory start date. Slot scarcity, not drug supply, has been the binding capacity constraint. |
| Bridging therapy | Interim treatment holding the disease stable while cells are manufactured. |
| CRS | Cytokine release syndrome — massive inflammatory response as the T cells activate; fever, low blood pressure, organ stress. Treated with tocilizumab and steroids. |
| ICANS | Immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome — confusion, aphasia, seizures. |
| REMS | FDA-mandated Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy: extra monitoring and site-certification requirements that restrict where a drug can be given. |
| scFv / D-Domain | The antibody-derived gripper in most CARs; Arcellx's D-Domain is a smaller non-antibody alternative. |
| Antigen escape | Tumour stops expressing the target marker. Drives BCMA → GPRC5D sequencing in myeloma. |
| In vivo CAR-T | Reprogramming T cells inside the body via lipid nanoparticle (tLNP) or viral delivery — no apheresis, no external factory. |
